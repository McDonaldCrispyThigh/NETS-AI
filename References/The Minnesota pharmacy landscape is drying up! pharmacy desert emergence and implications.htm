Abstract

The Minnesota pharmacy landscape is drying up! pharmacy desert emergence and implications

Chrystian Pereira, PharmD1, Olihe Okoro, PhD, MPH2, Anjoli Punjabi, PharmD, MPH3, Emily Styles, MPH4, James Peacock, PhD, MPH4 and Lindsay Sorge, PharmD, MPH1
(1)University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, (2)University of Minnesota, Duluth, MN, (3)MOBĒ, Minneapolis, MN, (4)Minnesota Department of Health, St. Paul, MN

APHA 2024 Annual Meeting and Expo

Background

Pharmacy deserts are geolocations where people have limited access to medications and other pharmacy-based services. Criteria used to define pharmacy deserts are mostly by proximity. With openings and closures of pharmacies, monitoring the emergence of pharmacy deserts is critical to support equitable medication access for underserved populations. Objective: To identify changes in pharmacy deserts in Minnesota (2009-2021).

Methods

Pharmacy locations from four time points between 2009-2021 were geocoded using Esri ArcGIS software. Driving distance from the population center to the nearest pharmacy was calculated at the census tract level. Census tracts were identified as pharmacy deserts where driving distance was >1 mile in the Minneapolis-St. Paul (MSP) urban core, >5 miles in other metro areas, and >15 miles in non-metro areas.

Results

The number of Minnesota census tracts classified as pharmacy deserts increased from 114 (2009) to 134 (2021), primarily in the MSP urban core. Between 2009-2021, 94 pharmacies opened and 76 pharmacies closed. While statewide, the percent of the population living in a pharmacy desert increased from 6.2% to 7.4%, it increased from 25.8% to 32.8% in MSP.

Conclusion

Pharmacy closures erode community trust. Pharmacy deserts reduce medication access, and adversely impact chronic disease-management and population health. While reimbursements for dispensing are uniform, root causes of pharmacy closures are multifactorial including social unrest, demographics, and social risk factors unique to different geolocations. Further understanding of predictors of pharmacy closure is needed. Interventions require community engagement and advocacy for statewide policy changes related to pharmacy practice and public health.
