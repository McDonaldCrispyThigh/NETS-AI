Background: Previous analyses measuring geographic access to community pharmacies (phar-
macy access) have relied solely on driving distance or time, without considering other modes of 
transportation such as walking and public transportation. This omission represents an important 
limitation, particularly in metropolitan areas where other transportation modes are regularly 
used, which potentially can lead to the underestimation of inequities in pharmacy access. 
Objectives: To investigate whether measuring pharmacy access based on driving underestimates 
inequities in access, given the fact that not everyone has access to a vehicle.
Methods: We used geographic information system analysis to measure pharmacy access. We 
selected the top 10 metropolitan and micropolitan statistical areas with the largest popula-
tion in the United States because of the feasibility and availability of different transportation 
modes in those areas.
Results: We found that across the top 10 populated U.S. areas and based on a 20-minute travel 
time, pharmacy access will be overestimated for approximately 702,708 and 2,430,764 in-
dividuals if we consider driving as the transportation mode instead of walking and public 
transportation, respectively.
Conclusion: Results suggest that measures of pharmacy access derived solely from driving 
time will underestimate inequities in pharmacy access, specifically in areas with a low rate of 
private vehicle ownership.

Background
Community pharmacies play a crucial role in the United 
States public health system. In addition to medication 
dispensing, they provide consultations, harm-reduction
services, health screenings, medication management services, 
vaccine administration, and access to nonprescription medi-
cations for symptomatic and supportive care and over-the-
counter tests and devices. 1-5 In a commercially insured 
population, patients visited community pharmacies almost 
twice as often as they visited their physicians. 6 This ratio was 
the same among Medicare beneficiaries as well. 7 Further-
more, national initiatives have focused on the delivery of 
enhanced pharmacy services that can reach diverse pop-
ulations through clinically integrated U.S. state networks of 
pharmacy providers. 8,9 Therefore, ensuring reliable commu-
nity pharmacy access for every American is essential to 
improving overall health outcomes and reducing health care 
disparities.

threatened by the trend of pharmacy closures in recent 
years. 10-12 Pharmacy closures have been associated with

proposed to describe pharmacy locations serving a popula-
tion that would otherwise be a pharmacy desert (i.e., areas 
with no access to a pharmacy within a defined travel time 
range). In other words, “keystone pharmacies” serve areas 
with low pharmacy access and should be protected from 
closing to prevent the formation of a pharmacy desert. 15 
However, pharmacy locations have continued to close 
throughout the United States. The threat of pharmacy 
closures has generated a need to quantify geographic access 
to community pharmacies (pharmacy access) across the 
country to identify and prevent disparate access to health 
care.
A nationwide study calculated the driving distance to the 
closest community pharmacy and found that, in 2020, 
almost 90% of the U.S. population lived within 5 miles 
(8.04 km) of one. 16 Another study showed that almost 80% of 
census tracts in the U.S. have access to at least one pharmacy 
per 10,000 people within a 10-minute drive time. 17 However, 
these existing measures of pharmacy access are solely based 
on driving distance or time, assuming that individuals have 
access to a private vehicle. This is an important limitation of 
existing research, particularly in metropolitan areas, where 
alternative modes of transportation (walking or public
transportation) are frequently used. Furthermore, from a 
health equity perspective, using only driving distance or time 
may underestimate inequities in pharmacy access, since so-
cially disadvantaged individuals and underrepresented 
racial/ethnic minority groups are less likely to own cars. 18-20 
In other words, when access is measured solely by driving 
distance or time, some areas may be mistakenly classified as 
having optimal pharmacy access, even though residents 
without access to a private vehicle may still face significant 
challenges.
Objective
The primary objective of this paper was to investigate 
whether measuring pharmacy access based on driving 
underestimates inequity in access, given the fact that not 
everyone has access to a vehicle.
Methods
Data
We acquired the location of open-door pharmacies from 
the National Council for Prescription Drug Programs (NCPDP)
- DataQ in 2020. 21 We defined open-door pharmacies as those 
listed in the NCPDP DataQ database with a provider type 
classified as retail/community, Indian Health Service, Veter-
ans Affairs, or compounding pharmacies, and with an 
immunizations-provided indicator marked as “Yes.” We ob-
tained population data, weighted population centroids, and 
census block group boundaries from the 2020 Decennial 
Census data and TIGER/Line. 22 Block group-level household 
vehicle availability (i.e., percentage of households with access 
to a private vehicle at the census block group level) statistics 
were extracted from the 2020 American Community Survey 
5-year estimate data profile. We also used the ArcGIS Street-
Map Premium, 23 which provided the street data necessary for 
the travel time calculations.
Population
Analyses focused on the 10 most populated U.S. metro-
politan areas, including Atlanta, Chicago, Dallas, Houston, Los 
Angeles, Miami, New York, Philadelphia, Phoenix, and 
Washington. These areas were selected from the metropolitan 
and micropolitan statistical areas with the largest population 
in the United States. 24 All census block groups and their 
populations within these 10 metropolitan areas were selected 
for the analysis. We selected metropolitan areas because of 
the feasibility and availability of different transportation 
modes in those areas. We extracted pharmacies within 
100 km of the metropolitan area and generated their 
40-minute drive time to ensure that the following calculation 
included all possible pharmacies and populations.
Spatial analysis
We used ArcGIS Online and ArcGIS StreetMap Premium 23 
geocoding locator to geocode the location of pharmacies (see 
Appendix A for more details). To measure pharmacy access, 
we used advanced geographic information system (GIS)

analysis and the enhanced 2-step floating catchment area 
(E2SFCA) method. E2SFCA is the most accurate and advanced 
method to measure geographic access to services, which 
considers population demand for services (e.g., pharmacies), 
supply capacity of pharmacies, and the interaction between 
supply and demand within a travel time range. 25,26 It also uses 
a continuous distance decay effect so that an increase in 
distance would lead to less utilization of services. Travel time 
was measured from the population-weighted centroids of 
census block groups to the geocoded location of community 
pharmacies for each transportation mode, with the starting 
time being 3:00 PM on a Friday. We selected this time as it is 
the busiest time for pharmacies in the United States. (based 
on data collected during the COVID-19 pandemic.) 27 
Population-weighted centroids allowed us to account for the 
population distribution within census block groups rather 
than simply estimating their geometric centers (see Appendix 
B for its calculations). 28 The walking and driving isochrones 
and routes were generated in ArcGIS Pro using the Arcpy.nax 
module, with ArcGIS StreetMap Premium (North America 
2023 Release 1) as the transportation network data source. 23 
Public transit-related spatial analyses and routing were con-
ducted via the Bing Maps Directions API. 29 Both network data 
sources perform analyses based on historical and predicted 
traffic data, speed limits, road direction, and turn restrictions 
(see Appendix C for more GIS details).
In the first step of the E2SFCA, we created a catchment area 
of 20 min around each community pharmacy using network 
analysis. Since there was no specific estimate for travel time 
to pharmacies, this travel time threshold was based on 
average travel time for health care visits, shopping, picking up 
or returning goods, and other family or personal errands 
based on the 2022 National Household Travel Survey data. 30 
Specifically, in Metropolitan Statistical Areas with a popula-
tion greater than 3 million, 75% of household trips had du-
rations no more than 20 minutes for purposes including 
health care visits, shopping, picking up or returning goods, 
and other family or personal errands. Then, we calculated a 
pharmacy-to-population ratio based on the population de-
mand within each catchment. In the second step, we created a 
catchment area with the same travel time used in step one for 
each population-weighted centroid of census block groups, 
searched for the pharmacies within that catchment area, and 
summed up their pharmacy-to-population ratios to represent 
pharmacy access at the census block group level. Ratios closer 
to 0 indicate lower accessibility. In each step, distance decay 
weights based on a Gaussian function were used for travel 
times, with populations close to a pharmacy assigned weights 
near 1 to reflect the attraction of low travel costs, whereas 
individuals located in more distant locations are assigned 
weights near 0 to discount the attraction due to higher 
impedance (see Appendix D for the distance decay function 
calculations).
We measured an access index ratio for each transportation 
mode using the E2SFCA method and multiplied them by 
10,000 to represent pharmacy access per 10,000 people. We 
reported the percentage of census block groups with access to 
more than one pharmacy per 10,000 people (a common 
approach in prior analysis 17 ) for each transportation mode. In 
addition, we identified block groups and their associated 
population with walking or public transportation access to
fewer than one pharmacy but driving access to more than one 
pharmacy per 10,000 people for each metropolitan area. Then 
we estimated the population of each metropolitan area that 
do not have a private vehicle whose pharmacy access would 
be overestimated if we assumed they used a private vehicle 
for transportation.
Results
We identified 61,715 open-door pharmacies in the United 
States. Of those, 23,090 pharmacies were located within the 
study area. Figure 1 shows the percentage of census block 
groups in each metropolitan area with access to at least one 
pharmacy per 10,000 people within a 20-minute travel time 
by mode of transportation (see Appendix E for the private 
vehicle ownership rates, population density, and the cities 
included in each metropolitan area). Results indicate a wider 
disparity in pharmacy access based on walking and public 
transportation compared to driving. Across all 10 metro-
politan areas, over 95% of the block groups had access to at 
least one pharmacy per 10,000 people within a 20-minute 
driving time. Access was considerably lower for public 
transit and walking transportation modes. Specifically, the 
proportion of block groups with access to at least one 
pharmacy per 10,000 people within a 20-minute travel time 
by public transit ranged from 31.5% in Atlanta metropolitan 
area to 76.7% in Miami metropolitan area. The Atlanta 
metropolitan area also had the lowest access based on 
walking, with only 68.3% of block groups having access to at 
least one pharmacy per 10,000 people within a 20-minute 
walking time.
Table 1 reports the proportion of block groups and popu-
lation in each metropolitan area that had access to fewer than 
one pharmacy per 10,000 people within a 20-minute walking 
time. For instance, in Atlanta metropolitan area, 34.9% of 
block groups or 2.3 million individuals had walking access to 
fewer than one pharmacy per 10,000 people. However, of 
those block groups with suboptimal pharmacy access based 
on walking time, 99.8% had access to more than one pharmacy 
per 10,000 people based on driving.
Table 2 reports the proportion of block groups and 
population in each metropolitan area with public trans-
portation access to fewer than one pharmacy per 10,000 
people within a 20-minute travel time. For instance, in 
Dallas metropolitan area, 60.8% of block groups or 5.3 
million individuals had access to fewer than one pharmacy 
per 10,000 people. However, all these block groups had 
access to more than one pharmacy per 10,000 people based 
on driving.
Considering that only some people in the identified block 
groups in Tables 1 and 2 do not have access to a vehicle, we 
estimated the population whose pharmacy access would be 
overestimated if we assumed they used a private vehicle for 
transportation. Table 3 shows the estimated population 
without a private vehicle and with access to fewer than one 
pharmacy by walking or public transportation, but access to 
more than one pharmacy by driving, per 10,000 people within 
a 20-minute travel time. We based this estimate on the pri-
vate vehicle ownership rates of the identified block groups in 
Tables 1 and 2.

used a private vehicle. This number varies across our study

our study area, ranging from 75,049 in the Miami metropolitan 
area to 786,830 in the New York City metropolitan area.
Discussion
Prior analysis on geographic access to community phar-
macies (pharmacy access) has solely focused on driving dis-
tance or time to pharmacies. 16,17 However, residents of 
metropolitan areas do not always own private vehicles, and 
they may rely on other modes of transportation such as 
walking or public transportation. In this paper, we used 
advanced GIS analysis to measure pharmacy access while 
accounting for 2 other modes of transportation individuals 
may use when accessing pharmacies. We investigated 
whether measuring pharmacy access based on driving over-
estimates inequity in access, given the fact that not everyone 
has access to a private vehicle.
We observed a greater disparity in the pharmacy access 
index based on walking and public transportation compared 
to driving across the top 10 most populated U.S. areas. For 
instance, in the Dallas metropolitan area, 98.0% of block
groups had driving access to at least one pharmacy per 10,000 
people within a 20-minute travel time, but only 81.8% and 
34.1% had similar access based on walking and public trans-
portation. Also, in the Atlanta metropolitan area, 99.7% of 
block groups had driving access to at least one pharmacy per 
10,000 people, while only 64.8% and 32.1% had similar access 
based on walking and public transportation, respectively. Our 
results suggest that pharmacy access in metropolitan areas is 
heavily dependent on the transportation mode. Therefore, 
relying solely on driving time to measure pharmacy access 
can significantly overestimate the actual pharmacy access for 
individuals who depend on other modes of transportation. 
This can lead to a misleading perception of how well com-
munities are served by pharmacies, and therefore, those 
communities may not receive needed pharmacy resources 
due to this misattribution.
We also found that in block groups with walking access to 
fewer than one pharmacy per 10,000 people, between 89.5% 
(in the Phoenix metropolitan area) and 99.9% (in the Dallas 
metropolitan area) had driving access to at least one phar-
macy per 10,000 people (see Table 1). In addition, we found 
that in block groups with public transportation access to 
fewer than one pharmacy per 10,000 people, between 97.5% 
(in the Phoenix metropolitan area) and 100% (in the Dallas 
metropolitan area) had driving access to at least one phar-
macy per 10,000 people (see Table 2). Together, these findings 
suggest that walking and public transportation access to 
pharmacies would be overestimated for some block groups if 
we used driving as the transportation mode. These findings 
also emphasize that pharmacy planning in metropolitan areas 
should consider populations without a private vehicle, as they 
may face greater challenges in accessing necessary health 
care provided through pharmacies.

In summary, overestimating pharmacy access for pop-
ulations in certain geographic areas may have unintentional 
yet consequential downstream effects. These effects may 
include a failure to open new pharmacies in metropolitan 
areas with a low rate of private vehicle ownership and high 
reliance on public transportation. Should areas of low phar-
macy access via public transportation identified in this paper 
remain unaddressed, disparate health outcomes may grow for 
populations already in need of additional healthcare 
resources. Secondly, pharmacy deserts will likely grow as 
pharmacy closures continue to rise as a result of unsustain-
able business models tied to low or negative reimbursement 
rates for prescription dispensing and limited opportunities to 
diversify reimbursement streams. As deserts grow, pharmacy 
access, even in metropolitan areas with good pharmacy ac-
cess based on driving, will likely decrease. This will leave 
millions of Americans with fewer options to obtain their
medications same day or receive enhanced services provided 
by pharmacists from brick and mortar locations.
Limitations
Our analysis is subject to a few limitations. First, the travel 
time threshold we used was based on average travel time for 
health care visits, shopping, picking up or returning goods, and 
other family or personal errands, as there was no specific es-
timate for travel time to pharmacies. Second, while biking is a 
common mode of transportation in metropolitan areas, we did 
not consider that in this paper. Third, public transit travel times 
were calculated for a single departure time (Friday at 3:00 PM), 
which may not fully reflect the variability in transit accessi-
bility. Unlike driving or walking, transit users often plan their 
trips around scheduled departures, and small shifts in depar-
ture time (e.g., leaving at 2:55 vs 3:00) can significantly alter

Conclusion
In this article, we measured pharmacy access in the 10 
most populated U.S. areas using different modes of trans-
portation. Results suggest that measures of pharmacy access 
derived solely on driving time will underestimate inequities 
in pharmacy access. Findings can inform urban planning and 
public health policies. Efforts to improve pharmacy access 
should consider different transportation modes to ensure that 
all community members have adequate access to pharmacies, 
specifically in areas with a low rate of private vehicle 
ownership.
