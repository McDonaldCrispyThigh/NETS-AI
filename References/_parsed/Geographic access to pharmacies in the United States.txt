Invited Commentary | Pharmacy and Clinical Pharmacology
Measuring Geographic Access to Pharmacies
Kelly E. Anderson, PhD, MPP; T. Joseph Mattingly II, PharmD, MBA, PhD
In this issue of JAMA Network Open, Mathis et al1 define a new measure of community pharmacy
access, which they coin as pharmacy vulnerability index and use to identify keystone pharmacies.
This research comes at a critical time when there are substantial concerns about the viability of
community pharmacies, documented in numerous news articles, and during a policy window when
there is bipartisan support in Congress in pursuing legislation to reform payments from pharmacy
benefit managers (PBMs) to community pharmacies.
Over the last decade, Qato et al2 and Guadamuz et al3 have led the charge on measuring access
to community pharmacies. In pioneering work, they developed a definition of pharmacy deserts
adapted from the concept of food deserts, focused on communities without access to a pharmacy
based on a predefined travel distance (varying based on urbanicity) and whether the community was
lower income.2 Through the definition of pharmacy deserts, they have identified communities most
adversely affected by a lack of pharmacy access.3 As geocoding capabilities have improved, there are
opportunities to further refine this definition.
Mathis et al1 make several key contributions toward refining our measurements and
understanding of pharmacy access. First, although a multifaceted social vulnerability index has been
widely applied in health disparities research, the authors apply the measure specifically on
community pharmacy access to anticipate potential deserts. This adds to the pharmacy desert
construct by introducing a way to flag areas of concern, describing areas relying on a single pharmacy
as areas with a keystone pharmacy. Second, they incorporate drive times into their index, which adds
to a traditional distance-focused measure, as urban and rural travel may vary substantially in terms
of time. Third, this measurement identifies that when it comes to race and ethnicity disparities, White
populations in small rural areas may be at risk when we consider the heavy reliance on a single
pharmacy.
As 2024 drew to a close, Congress introduced and advanced several pieces of legislation that
would substantially change the relationship between PBMs and community pharmacies with the goal
of improving payments to community pharmacies. PBMs are entities hired by health plan sponsors
(eg, employers) to develop formularies, manage prescription utilization, contract with pharmacies to
form a pharmacy network for beneficiaries, and negotiate manufacturer rebates for the brand-
name drugs included on the formulary.4 Legislative proposals included reforms such as prohibiting a
parent company of a PBM or health insurer from also owning pharmacies, requiring that PBMs pass
through all rebates from drug manufacturers to health insurers, and setting minimum
reimbursement amounts to pharmacies based on the amount the PBM charges to the insurer.
Although these bills and provisions did not pass into law during the lame-duck session, there was
clear bipartisan interest in legislation to support access to prescription medications through
community pharmacies, which is likely to carry forward into the new Congress.
Mathis and colleagues1 estimate 8.9% of the population lives in a community with a single
keystone pharmacy. In communities with limited pharmacy access, enticing new pharmacies to open
in pharmacy deserts requires different policy solutions than policies that fight to keep pharmacies
open in at-risk communities. Prior to this study, we did not have a measure to identify the at-risk
communities sitting on the precipice of losing pharmacy access. Yet emerging studies show that
when any community pharmacy closes, medication adherence for key drug classes such as
anticonvulsants and cardiovascular medications is significantly reduced and mail-order pharmacy

While broad PBM reform may improve the financial status of community pharmacies across the
country, if we have a policy goal of preventing at-risk communities from becoming pharmacy deserts,
more targeted policy proposals to support keystone pharmacies may be needed. This could take the
form of federal legislation or could be implemented by states with a disproportionately high number
of communities relying on keystone pharmacies. Mathis et al1 make several recommendations for
this type of targeted legislation such as increasing dispensing fees for keystone pharmacies. Another
model could be a critical access pharmacy model adapted from the existing critical access
hospital model.7
The approaches developed by Mathis and colleagues1 also offer a template to improve
pharmacy network adequacy standards. For the Medicare and Tricare programs, pharmacy network
adequacy standards are set using travel distance thresholds by county urbanicity. This study offers
an approach to instead assess the adequacy of pharmacy networks based on pharmacy availability
and typical travel patterns in each community.
While the pharmacy landscape is changing with an increasing reliance on mail-order pharmacies
and the introduction of innovative pharmacy models, such as Mark Cuban Cost Plus Drug Company,
community pharmacies play a vital role in medication access and the health of communities. The
research by Mathis et al1 is a key methodologic advancement in allowing researchers and
policymakers to identify communities where community pharmacy access is at risk.
