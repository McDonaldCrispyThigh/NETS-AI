Geospatial Artificial Intelligence (GeoAI) Is
Widening the Digital Divide
Xiao Huang
Department of Environmental Sciences, Emory University, USA
Geospatial artificial intelligence (GeoAI), merging AI with spatial data, offers potent new ways to understand
and shape our world. Yet, its ascent threatens to carve a deeper digital divide, moving beyond access gaps to
fundamental inequities in data sovereignty, computational power, and the critical literacy required to navigate
GeoAI’s complexities. This concentration of capability risks entrenching power, enabling the privileged to
dominate spatial narratives and decisions driven by these technologies. This work dissects how GeoAI
exacerbates
existing
socioeconomic,
educational,
and
infrastructural
divides,
examining
the
profound
implications for diverse communities. It argues that mitigation demands more than technological fixes; it
necessitates inclusive policy frameworks, equitable resource distribution, and the deliberate cultivation of
widespread GeoAI literacy. Failure risks a future where GeoAI amplifies injustice, demanding proactive
strategies now to harness its power for genuinely equitable ends and shared societal benefit, rather than
reinforcing existing fractures. Key Words: big data era, digital divide, GeoAI, geospatial data.

of GeoAI, extends significantly beyond mere techno-
logical access. It critically encompasses disparities in
accessing high-quality data, the availability of robust
computational resources, and the requisite critical

advanced tools. Individuals and communities lacking

Understanding the Digital Divide
The term digital divide refers to the significant dispar-
ity in access to and utilization of digital technologies,
a growing global issue in the twenty-first century
(Cullen 2001). This divide encompasses not only the
gap between those with and without Internet access
but also disparities in digital literacy, skills, and the
ability to benefit from digital technologies. With the
increasing implementation of e-government services
and digital platforms by governments and organizations
worldwide, concerns about the digital divide’s impact
on the equitable delivery of these services has become
more pronounced (Belanger and Carter 2008).
The digital divide is a complex phenomenon
rooted in broader socioeconomic and geographical
inequalities. It involves more than just access to
hardware and infrastructure; it includes the ways
people communicate and access information through
various digital channels like mobile phones, televi-
sion, and the Internet. This highlights the impor-
tance
of
considering
the
social
and
political
dimensions of the digital divide, as well as the vary-
ing levels of access and digital literacy among people
from different backgrounds and skill levels (Fuchs
and Horak 2008).
Socioeconomic inequality is a primary driver of the
digital divide. Individuals from lower income house-
holds are less likely to afford computers, smartphones,
and Internet subscriptions, limiting their participation
in the digital space, access to information, and benefits
from online education and services (Van Deursen and
Van Dijk 2011). This disparity is particularly pro-
nounced in the big data era, where a significant amount
of data is contributed by individuals who actively par-
ticipate in the digital space, such as through mobile
Global Positioning System (GPS) points and social
media. Consequently, when scholars analyze these data,
the experiences and contributions of those from these
groups
are
often
overlooked
(Li
et
al.
2024).
Geographical location also significantly affects digital
access, with rural and remote areas often experiencing
inadequate infrastructure, slower Internet speeds, or a
complete lack of connectivity. This geographical digital
divide exacerbates regional inequalities, hindering eco-
nomic development and access to digital services in
these areas (Halford and Savage 2010).
Educational attainment is closely linked to digital
literacy. Those with higher education levels are gen-
erally more proficient in using digital technologies
and better equipped to leverage the Internet for
information, employment, and social engagement.
Conversely, individuals with lower education levels
might struggle with basic digital tasks, further deep-
ening the divide (DiMaggio and Hargittai 2001).
Age is another critical factor, with older adults being
less likely to use digital technologies compared to
younger generations. This age-related digital divide
is due to a lack of familiarity and comfort with tech-
nology, as well as physical limitations that make using
digital devices challenging (Hunsaker and Hargittai
2018). Cultural and linguistic barriers also contribute,
as people from minority groups or those who speak
minority languages might find fewer online resources
available in their language, hindering their full partici-
pation in the digital world. This underscores the need
for culturally and linguistically inclusive digital content
(Fantin and Girardello 2008).
The advent of GeoAI—the integration of geographic
information systems (GIS) with AI—significantly shifts
the digital landscape. The GeoAI era demands new
knowledge, advanced modeling, training skills, and
robust Internet facilities. As these technologies become
integral to various sectors, the digital divide risks being
further exacerbated. The need for computational resour-
ces and sophisticated digital skills will become even
more critical, potentially widening the gap between
those with access and those without. In this article, I
aim to dissect how the GeoAI era, combined with the
big data revolution, intensifies the digital divide, and
then discuss strategies to overcome this challenge.

Unequal Data: Access and Quality Challenges in
the GeoAI Era
In the GeoAI era, access to high-quality data is of
great importance. Disparities in data availability,
however, can intensify the digital divide. Regions
with limited Internet infrastructure and lower tech-
nological investment often lack the requisite data
repositories and the capacity to effectively collect,
store, and manage geospatial data. For example, rural
areas in developing countries might not have access
to satellite imagery or comprehensive geospatial data
sets due to inadequate Internet connectivity and
financial constraints (Turner et al. 2015). In some
parts of sub-Saharan Africa, limited access to high-
resolution satellite data hinders efforts to monitor
agricultural productivity and optimize crop yields.

geospatial data can lead to inadequate disaster pre-
paredness
and
recovery
strategies,
exacerbating
regional vulnerability and economic instability.
The High Cost of Precision: Computational
Resource Inequities
A critical dimension of the digital divide in the
GeoAI era stems from profound inequities in access-
ing the substantial computational power required for
advanced modeling and analysis. High-performance
computing (HPC) resources and specialized exper-
tise, essential for processing large data sets and train-
ing
sophisticated
GeoAI
models,
are
typically
concentrated within well-funded institutions and
technologically advanced regions, creating significant
barriers elsewhere.
The practical implications are stark. For example,
developing robust GeoAI models for complex envi-
ronmental tasks, such as predicting flood zones by
integrating
diverse inputs like
weather
patterns,
elevation, historical data, and social sensing feeds,
necessitates
considerable
computational
capacity
often unavailable to smaller municipalities or organi-
zations in developing countries (Huang, Wang, and
Li 2018a, 2018b; Bulti and Abebe 2020; Song et al.
2023). Similarly, exploiting the insights from “big
social
data”—leveraging
vast
streams
of
user-
generated content like social media or mobility
records—often depends on access to HPC environ-
ments, limiting participation in this burgeoning area
of
GeoAI
application
(Bello-Orgaz,
Jung,
and
Camacho 2016).
These
limitations
acutely
affect
research
and
innovation capacity in resource-constrained settings.
Many universities and institutions in regions like
Africa lack the funding or infrastructure for adequate
HPC facilities, directly hindering their ability to
engage in cutting-edge GeoAI research or develop
tailored solutions for local challenges (Mzuza and
Van der Westhuizen 2020). This resource gap is
poised to widen further with the increasing computa-
tional demands of newer technologies; the training
requirements
for
foundational
generative
AI
(GenAI) models, for instance, significantly surpass
those of many existing GeoAI models (Fui-Hoon
Nah et al. 2023; Wang, Hu, et al. 2024). Notably,
such computational barriers are not exclusive to
developing nations. Even within European countries
possessing
relatively
strong
economies,
persistent
challenges include insufficient funding for crucial
GIS
equipment
updates
(e.g.,
Greece,
Iceland),
shortages of computers and multimedia resources
(e.g., Slovenia), and unfavorable student-to-equip-
ment ratios (e.g., Romania), underscoring the wide-
spread nature of computational resource inequities in
the GeoAI landscape (Donert 2005).

advanced
knowledge
in
both
fields,
along
with
strong
analytical
and
computational
skills. Regions with underfunded educational institu-

putting their populations at a significant disadvan-

Governing the Divide: Policy Implications and
Governance Challenges
The implementation of GeoAI technologies is
often hindered by policy and governance challenges,
especially in regions with weak institutional frame-
works. Effective utilization of GeoAI necessitates
robust policies that encourage data sharing, privacy
protection, and equitable access to technological
resources (Ghafoor and Tahir 2023; Mortaheb and
Jankowski 2023). In many developing countries,
though, the lack of comprehensive geospatial poli-
cies and regulatory frameworks leads to fragmented
data governance and limited collaboration between
governmental and nongovernmental organizations.
For example, in several African nations, the absence
of standardized policies for geospatial data collection
and usage creates significant obstacles to integrating
GeoAI into national development plans (Osuteye,
Johnson, and Brown 2017). Furthermore, bureau-
cratic inefficiencies and corruption can impede the
deployment of GeoAI solutions, as observed in some
developing countries where resource allocation for
technological
infrastructure
is
often
mismanaged
(Akinwale
2010).
Another
example
is
India’s
National Spatial Data Infrastructure (NSDI), which
was designed to establish uniform metadata standards
but faces challenges due to its top-down, data-centric
approach,
limiting
bottom-up
involvement
and
responsiveness to local needs. The diverse legacy
processes of mapping agencies further hinder the
adoption of unified standards and open-data norms,
creating resistance and inefficiencies (Singh 2005).
These governance and standardization issues largely
delay the adoption of GeoAI, perpetuating existing
inequalities by failing to address the specific needs of
marginalized communities. Without coherent policy
frameworks, the potential of GeoAI to drive socio-
economic development and environmental sustain-
ability remains largely untapped, thereby reinforcing
the digital divide.
Cultural Disconnect: Overcoming Sociocultural
Barriers in GeoAI Access
Sociocultural
barriers
also
affect
the
unequal
adoption of GeoAI technologies. Cultural attitudes
toward technology, levels of digital literacy, and
societal norms can influence the acceptance and uti-
lization of GeoAI across various regions. In some tra-
ditional societies, resistance to new technologies
arises due to fears of job displacement or mistrust of
automated systems. For instance, in certain rural
communities in Asia, farmers who rely on traditional
agricultural practices might oppose the introduction
of GeoAI in AI-driven agricultural interventions,
remaining skeptical of their effectiveness (Raj et al.
2020). In parts of Africa, low levels of digital liter-
acy can hinder the effective use of GeoAI tools for
urban planning or disaster management (Choi, Dutz,
and Usman 2020). Furthermore, political environ-
ments can limit access to and use of emerging tech-
nologies.
For example,
in Afghanistan,
unstable
governance, ongoing conflicts, and limited policy
support have severely undermined information tech-
nology and other critical infrastructure essential for
high-tech
applications
(Kumar
2021).
This
has
unquestionably hindered the adoption of AI, includ-
ing GeoAI. These sociocultural challenges are often
exacerbated by gender disparities, where women and
girls have less access to education and technological
resources compared to men (Antonio and Tuffley
2014).

Strategies to Bridge the Digital Divide in
the GeoAI Era
To effectively address the digital divide exacer-
bated by the advent of GeoAI, a comprehensive and
multifaceted approach is required. The following
strategies highlight critical areas for intervention and
policy development to ensure that the benefits of
GeoAI are accessible to all, thereby promoting equi-
table socioeconomic development and technological
inclusion.
Investment in Digital Infrastructure
The deployment and utilization of GeoAI funda-
mentally depend on robust digital infrastructure, yet
access
remains
deeply
uneven.
Prioritizing
the
expansion of reliable, high-speed Internet to under-
served rural and remote regions is not merely about
connectivity; it is about laying the groundwork for
digital citizenship and participation in an increas-
ingly data-driven world (Wang 2010; Xia 2022).
This requires significant public and private invest-
ment in foundational technologies like fiber-optic
networks.
Where
terrestrial
infrastructure
proves
logistically or economically prohibitive, alternative
solutions such as advanced wireless networks and
satellite constellations become crucial.
Furthermore,
the
computational
demands
of
GeoAI—processing vast data sets and training com-
plex models—necessitate access to HPC resources.
For many institutions and communities, particularly
in the Global South, building and maintaining local
HPC clusters is infeasible. Here, cloud computing
platforms (e.g., AWS, Azure, Google Cloud) offer a
potential pathway to democratization, providing scal-
able, pay-as-you-go access to computational power
and specialized geospatial tools (Wang 2010). Yet,
reliance
on
cloud
infrastructure
introduces
new
dependencies and potential vulnerabilities, raising
questions about data sovereignty and the long-term
costs
associated
with
access
versus
ownership.
Therefore, infrastructure strategies must consider not
only access but also digital autonomy and resilience.
Public–private partnerships can be effective mecha-
nisms for funding and deployment, but they must be
structured to prioritize public good and equitable
access over purely commercial interests. Ultimately,
I would argue that viewing digital infrastructure less
as a market commodity and more as an essential
public utility, akin to roads or electricity, is critical
for bridging the GeoAI divide.
Affordable Access to Digital Technologies
Beyond infrastructure, the tangible tools of the
digital age—devices, software, and data—must be
accessible. Financial barriers significantly limit par-
ticipation; the cost of capable computers, smart-
phones, and critically, data plans, can be prohibitive
for low-income households (Van Deursen and Van
Dijk 2011). Targeted subsidies, voucher programs, or
universal basic service initiatives can mitigate these
costs, enabling individuals to acquire the necessary
hardware. Affordability extends beyond the initial
purchase,
however; ongoing
data
costs, software
licenses (although mitigated by open-source alterna-
tives like QGIS and Python libraries), and the “time
cost”
of
learning
represent
significant
hurdles
(Schradie 2011).
Public–private partnerships, such as those exem-
plified by initiatives like Google’s AI for Social
Good or the Gates Foundation’s investments (Lai
2021), can channel resources toward underresourced
regions. Their impact, however, must be critically
assessed for sustainability and alignment with local
needs, avoiding technological solutionism (Morozov
2013). Community technology centers and libraries
play a vital role, offering shared access not only to
devices and the Internet but potentially to more spe-
cialized software and expertise (Grunwald et al.
2007; Khmelevsky et al. 2011). These spaces can
evolve into hubs for collaborative learning and par-
ticipatory GeoAI projects, integrating local knowl-
edge with advanced tools. Nonetheless, it is crucial
to recognize the limitations; shared access points
might
not
suffice
for
computationally
intensive
GeoAI tasks or provide the privacy needed for sensi-
tive projects. Ensuring equitable access requires a
multipronged approach that addresses the full spec-
trum of costs—financial, temporal, and cognitive—
associated with engaging meaningfully with GeoAI.

Inclusive Policy and Governance Frameworks
Bridging the digital divide in the GeoAI era
necessitates proactive and carefully crafted policy
and governance frameworks that move beyond mere
technical
considerations
to
embed
principles
of
equity and justice. Simply advocating for data shar-
ing is insufficient; the terms of collection, access,
use, and control are paramount, especially given the
potential for GeoAI to amplify existing societal
inequalities (Elish 2019). Effective governance must
establish clear, enforceable, and ethically grounded
rules for the entire geospatial data life cycle.
Privacy protection demands particular attention
in the geospatial context due to the inherent loca-
tional sensitivity and the heightened risk of reidenti-
fication, even from supposedly anonymized data sets
(De Montjoye et al. 2013). Policies must incorporate
stringent data protection principles, drawing inspira-
tion
from
comprehensive
frameworks
like
the
European Union (EU) General Data Protection
Regulation (GDPR), including data minimization
(collecting only necessary data), purpose limitation
(using data only for specified purposes), and robust
security measures (Voigt and Von Dem Bussche
2017). Critically, this involves defining clear proto-
cols for obtaining meaningful informed consent,
especially
when
dealing
with
collective
or
community-level data, and respecting principles of
Indigenous
data
sovereignty
where
applicable.
Predeployment
algorithmic
impact
assessments
should become standard practice to anticipate and
mitigate potential discriminatory outcomes (Gupta,
An, and Shekhar 2023). Furthermore, policies must
be intentionally designed to counteract the perpetua-
tion and amplification of historical and systemic
biases often encoded within geospatial data (e.g., leg-
acies of redlining, infrastructure disparities, or under-
representation of marginalized groups in volunteered
geographic information;
Laituri 2002; O’Sullivan
2006). This requires going beyond simple nondiscri-
mination clauses to mandate proactive measures,
such as fairness audits of GeoAI models and ensuring
data sets adequately represent diverse populations
(Mehrabi et al. 2022).
Promoting Cultural and Linguistic Inclusion
To ensure GeoAI technologies are truly inclusive
and bridge rather than widen the digital divide, pro-
active strategies must address deep-seated cultural
and linguistic barriers. This requires moving beyond
superficial measures toward fundamentally rethinking
how
GeoAI
tools,
data,
and
applications
are
designed, deployed, and governed in diverse socio-
cultural contexts (Tsou and Smith 2011).
Merely translating interfaces or documentation
into multiple languages, although necessary, is insuf-
ficient. Linguistic inclusion demands attention to
dialects, varying levels of technical literacy, and the
potential absence of equivalent technical terms in
all languages, often necessitating collaborative termi-
nology development. The pervasive dominance of
English
within
the
GeoAI
field—from
software
menus to foundational research papers—creates sig-
nificant barriers to entry and participation for non-
English speakers, reinforcing existing global power
imbalances in knowledge production. Furthermore,
cultural appropriateness in GeoAI extends signifi-
cantly
beyond
language.

involves
critically

examining how spatial phenomena are represented,
as cartographic choices regarding symbology, color

alienating communities but can lead to extractive
practices that undermine trust and the potential for
equitable GeoAI deployment.
Supporting Local Innovations and Capacity
Building
Although external support can provide initial
resources, bridging the GeoAI divide sustainably
necessitates the cultivation of endogenous innova-
tion capabilities; that is, fostering innovation that
arises from within local contexts and is responsive to
specific regional challenges. Supporting local GeoAI
startups and entrepreneurs requires more than just
capital; it demands mentorship sensitive to the
unique socioeconomic and infrastructural landscapes
they navigate. Incubators and accelerators can be
valuable, but their efficacy hinges on moving beyond
replicating Silicon Valley archetypes toward fostering
“appropriate technology” rooted in local needs and
resource availability. The goal should be to build
resilient local innovation ecosystems where knowl-
edge generation, application, and economic value
creation reinforce each other.
Capacity building, therefore, must transcend rudi-
mentary technical training on specific software pack-
ages. It must aim to cultivate a deeper critical
GeoAI literacy—the ability not only to use the tools
but to understand their theoretical underpinnings,
evaluate their outputs critically, recognize inherent
biases in data and algorithms, and navigate the com-
plex ethical terrain of geospatial data (Elwood and
Leszczynski 2013). Training programs need curricula
co-designed with local stakeholders to ensure rele-
vance to specific environmental, social, or economic
challenges. Pedagogical approaches should prioritize
problem-based learning and connect skills develop-
ment directly to tangible local opportunities to miti-
gate the risk of “brain drain,” where newly skilled
individuals leave due to a lack of local application
pathways. Establishing sustainable training infrastruc-
tures, perhaps embedded within local universities or
community centers rather than relying solely on
short-term external projects, is crucial for long-term
impact
and
accessibility
(Ashraf,
Grunfeld,
and
Quazi 2015). Ultimately, the objective is not just to
create users of GeoAI, but to empower communities
to become critical creators, adaptors, and governors
of these powerful technologies within their own
contexts.
Gender Equality and Empowerment Programs
The digital divide within the GeoAI era is not
gender-neutral; it reflects and often amplifies deep-
seated societal inequities that systematically disad-
vantage women and girls, particularly those at the
intersections
of
multiple
marginalizations
(e.g.,
based on race, class, location). Addressing this
requires moving beyond surface-level interventions
toward tackling the structural barriers, cultural
biases, and power dynamics that limit women’s
meaningful participation and leadership in the
GeoAI field. Initiatives like scholarships and men-
torship programs can provide crucial support for
individuals (Riedler et al. 2021), but they primar-
ily address symptoms rather than the root causes
embedded within educational pathways, workplace
cultures, and the very design of geospatial technol-
ogies. A truly transformative approach necessitates
systemic change.
Targeted
training
programs,
if
thoughtfully
designed, can help bridge specific competency gaps
(Franklin et al. 2023). Their effectiveness, though,
depends on adopting gender-responsive pedagogies
that actively counter stereotype threat and foster
critical engagement, rather than merely transferring

technical skills. Crucially, such programs must inte-
grate perspectives from feminist critiques of science
and technology, enabling participants to recognize
and challenge gender biases potentially encoded
within geospatial data sets (e.g., gendered patterns in
volunteered
geographic
information)
and
GeoAI
algorithms themselves. Without this critical lens,
even technically proficient women could find their
work
inadvertently
perpetuating
existing
spatial
inequalities.
Ultimately,
sustainable
progress
demands
con-
fronting the often exclusionary cultures prevalent in
many science, technology, engineering, and mathe-
matics (STEM) fields, including geospatial science
and AI development. This requires organizations to
move beyond tokenistic diversity measures toward
implementing robust, evidence-based policies that
promote equitable hiring and promotion, ensure
equal pay, foster genuinely inclusive and harassment-
free work environments, and support work–life inte-
gration
(Bilimoria
and
Liang
2012).
The
goal
extends beyond mere representation; it is about
empowering women as cocreators and critical stake-
holders in steering GeoAI toward more equitable,
socially just, and contextually relevant outcomes
for all.
Community-Driven Approaches
To ensure GeoAI initiatives genuinely contribute
to equitable development rather than reinforcing
existing
divides,
community-driven
approaches
rooted in the principles of participatory GIS are
essential (Weiner, Harris, and Craig 2002; Dunn
2007; Huang, Wang, Lu, et al. 2024). This necessi-
tates moving fundamentally beyond superficial con-
sultation
or
extractive
data
collection
toward
processes that center community agency, knowledge,
and decision-making power throughout the entire
GeoAI life cycle—from problem definition and data
generation to analysis, application design, and gover-
nance. A primary challenge lies in navigating the
inherent power dynamics between external experts
(i.e., researchers, developers, and policymakers) and
community members, as well as inequalities within
communities themselves (Cooke and Kothari 2001).
Without deliberate strategies to foster trust, ensure
transparency, and actively amplify marginalized voi-
ces, participatory processes risk becoming tokenistic
exercises that legitimize externally driven agendas or
are captured by local elites, failing to empower those
most affected.
Therefore, truly community-driven GeoAI must
strive toward genuine coproduction, where commu-
nity members are partners in defining research ques-
tions, designing methodologies, interpreting results,
and determining how technologies are deployed
(McCall and Minang 2005; Huang 2024; Huang,
Wang, Yang, et al. 2024). This aligns with advanc-
ing principles of community data sovereignty, assert-
ing
the
rights
of
communities
to
govern
the
collection, ownership, access, analysis, interpretation,
and use of data relating to their members, lands, and
resources. Operationalizing this involves establishing
clear, community-defined protocols for data gover-
nance,
obtaining
meaningful
collective
informed
consent that addresses the complexities of AI appli-
cations,
ensuring
equitable
benefit
sharing,
and
developing long-term community data stewardship
models (Carroll et al. 2023). By embedding GeoAI
development within frameworks of community con-
trol and reflexive participatory practice, it becomes
possible to harness these technologies not merely for
efficiency but as tools for empowerment, social learn-
ing, and advancing locally determined visions of a
just and sustainable future.
Conclusion
GeoAI stands as a powerful testament to human
ingenuity, offering unprecedented capabilities to ana-
lyze, interpret, and potentially reshape our world.
Yet, this very power carries profound risks. As
explored throughout this article, the fusion of AI
with spatial data threatens not merely to replicate,
but to significantly deepen the digital divide, trans-
forming it from a gap in access to a chasm in under-
standing, capability, and ultimately, agency. The
danger lies in creating a future where the ability to
leverage GeoAI becomes a new form of geopolitical
and socioeconomic power, accessible only to the
few, leaving vast populations subject to spatial narra-
tives and decisions crafted without their input or
understanding.
Bridging this emergent divide requires more than
infrastructural investment or basic skills training. It
demands a fundamental shift toward fostering critical
GeoAI literacy—the capacity not just to use these
tools, but to question their assumptions, interrogate

into the fabric of the field, and embedding ethical
