ABSTRACT  
The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has 
sparked growing interest in their potential applications within 
urban planning. These models offer novel capabilities in natural 
language processing tasks, potentially providing advanced 
support across various aspects of urban planning. Despite the 
promises, little is known about how and to what extent LLMs have 
been applied in the planning literature. This study addresses this 
gap by conducting a comprehensive literature review using the 
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta- 
Analyses (PRISMA) methodology. The review depicts five key 
domains where LLMs are being applied in urban planning: (a) 
planning and management; (b) public services and participation; 
(c) 
transportation 
and 
urban 
mobility; 
(d) 
environmental 
monitoring and sustainability; and (e) urban design and 
architecture. Additionally, the review identifies four essential 
characteristics that LLMs should possess to be effective in urban 
planning: (a) professionalism; (b) inclusivity; (c) trustworthiness; and 
(e) convenience. Considering the findings, a conceptual framework 
is developed that illustrates how these characteristics enhance the 
flexibility and effectiveness of LLMs in supporting urban planning 
tasks. This framework offers a theoretical foundation for urban 
planners to effectively integrate LLMs into practice and provides a 
roadmap for future research and technological innovations in the 
application of LLMs within urban environments.

paradigms and conceptual frameworks, reflecting cyclical waves of technological inno­
vation over decades (Yigitcanlar et al., 2025). In recent years, the integration of artificial 
intelligence (AI) into urban planning has emerged as an important research frontier,

Urban planning, which is defined as a systematic process of thinking through commu­
nity issues, evaluating alternative courses of action, and selecting and implementing sol­
utions that promote health, safety, economic well-being, and environmental quality 
(Levy et al., 2024), has historically evolved in response to technological innovations. 
From the introduction of geographic information systems (GIS) in the 1960s (Goodchild, 
2023) to the emergence of participatory planning technologies in the digital era (Foth, 
2011), the discipline has continuously integrated new tools to address increasingly 
complex urban challenges.
Recent studies have documented LLM applications across various urban planning 
domains. In strategic planning, LLMs have been employed for policy document analysis, 
trend identification, and scenario development (Wang, Pang et al., 2024; Li, Xia et al., 
2024). Transportation planning applications include traffic pattern analysis, public 
transit optimization, and mobility demand forecasting (Zhang, Fu et al., 2024; Tian 
et al., 2023). Environmental planning has seen LLM applications in sustainability assess­
ment, climate change adaptation planning, and environmental impact analysis (Hao 
et al., 2024; Chen, Yao et al., 2024). Public participation represents another significant 
application area, where LLMs facilitate multilingual community engagement, automate 
feedback analysis, and support inclusive planning processes (Fu et al., 2024; Zhou, Lin 
et al., 2024). Urban design applications include spatial layout optimization, regulatory 
compliance checking, and design alternative generation (Liang et al., 2024; Zhang, 
Xiang et al., 2024).
These emerging applications reflect contemporary urban planning’s evolution across 
multiple interconnected domains, such as strategic management, public engagement, 
transportation systems, environmental sustainability, and urban design, as discussed in 
diverse scholarly works (Campbell, 2013; Hall, 2014; Alexander, 2016). Each domain pre­
sents unique analytical challenges requiring sophisticated data processing capabilities, 
stakeholder coordination, and long-term strategic thinking that have traditionally 
relied on human expertise, institutional knowledge, and established planning method­
ologies (Healey, 2006; Allmendinger, 2017).
The application of digital technologies in urban contexts has undergone significant 
conceptual and practical evolution over the past two decades. The initial smart cities 
movement, emerging in the early 2000s, focused primarily on optimizing urban 
systems through Internet of Things (IoT) technologies, sensor networks, and data ana­
lytics platforms (Hollands, 2008; Kitchin, 2014). These approaches emphasized 
efficiency, predictability, and centralized control, reinforcing what Vanolo (2014) 
describes as a disciplinary strategy for governing cities through data-driven decision 
making.
Nevertheless, recent scholarship has identified significant limitations in traditional 
smart city approaches, particularly their technocratic orientation, limited citizen engage­
ment, and tendency toward technological determinism (Cardullo and Kitchin, 2019). 
These critiques have contributed to the emergence of what several scholars term “AI 
urbanism” (Cugurullo, 2020; Caprotti et al., 2024), representing a paradigmatic shift 
from automation-focused smart cities toward more autonomous, adaptive urban 
systems.

AI urbanism differs from smart urbanism in several key aspects. While smart cities 
emphasize real-time data processing and system optimization, AI urbanism introduces 
autonomous decision-making capabilities that can operate with varying degrees of 
human oversight (Milakis et al., 2017; Batty, 2018; Cugurullo, 2020). This transition illus­
trates a shift from using digital technologies as tools for optimization toward embedding 
autonomous, learning systems into urban governance (Batty, 2018; Cugurullo, 2020).
Recent research has characterized this evolution through multiple theoretical lenses. 
Urban political economists examine how AI urbanism reproduces or challenges existing 
power structures in city governance (Leszczynski, 2020). Urban geographers investigate 
the spatial implications of AI deployment, particularly regarding accessibility, equity, and 
spatial justice (Safransky, 2019; Fields, 2022). Meanwhile, urban planning scholars focus 
on the professional and methodological implications of integrating AI tools into plan­
ning practice (Qian et al., 2023; He et al., 2025).
Within this broader context of AI urbanism, large language models (LLM) represent a 
distinct category of urban AI. Unlike earlier AI applications that focused primarily on 
numerical data processing or image recognition, LLMs excel in natural language under­
standing, text generation, and complex reasoning tasks that closely align with traditional 
planning activities (Zhang, Fu et al., 2024; Chen, Yao et al., 2024). Their ability to process 
extensive textual information, synthesize diverse data sources, and generate human-read­
able outputs positions them as potentially transformative tools for urban planning 
practice.
LLMs’ training on vast corpora of text enables them to draw upon extensive knowl­
edge bases spanning multiple disciplines relevant to urban planning, including econ­
omics, environmental science, transportation engineering, and social policy (Brown 
et al., 2020; Chowdhery et al., 2023). This interdisciplinary knowledge integration 
capacity aligns well with urban planning’s inherently cross-sectoral nature and its 
requirement for synthesizing insights from multiple domains of expertise. Additionally, 
their technical capabilities—including proficiency in multilingual processing, capacity 
for handling incomplete information, and ability to generate contextually appropriate 
responses—address several longstanding challenges in planning practice, such as synthe­
sizing information from multiple sources, engaging diverse stakeholders, and communi­
cating complex concepts to broad audiences (Forester, 1999; Healey, 2006).
Nonetheless, these applications also reveal significant challenges that require careful 
consideration. Technical limitations include issues of accuracy, bias, and reliability 
when processing complex urban data (Li, Haider et al., 2024; Wang and Zhong, 2024). 
Methodological challenges involve questions of appropriate integration with existing 
planning workflows, quality control mechanisms, and professional accountability 
(Cugurullo and Xu, 2024). Ethical considerations encompass concerns about algorithmic 
bias, democratic participation, and the potential displacement of human judgment in 
critical planning decisions (Bozkir et al., 2024; Jiao et al., 2024).
Despite growing interest in LLM applications within urban planning, current research 
remains fragmented and lacks a comprehensive theoretical framework for understanding 
how these technologies can be effectively integrated into planning practice. Most existing 
studies focus on specific technical applications without addressing broader questions of 
professional integration, methodological appropriateness, or long-term implications for 
planning practice and democratic governance.

This fragmentation reflects what Palmini and Cugurullo (2023) and Markus et al. 
(2025) identify as broader challenges in urban AI research: the field’s rapid technological 
development has outpaced theoretical and methodological reflection, resulting in appli­
cation-driven research that lacks sufficient critical analysis of fundamental assumptions 
and implications. Furthermore, the interdisciplinary nature of both urban planning and 
AI development has led to research efforts that often operate within disciplinary silos, 
limiting opportunities for comprehensive understanding (Palmini and Cugurullo, 
2023; Markus et al., 2025).
The present study addresses this research gap by developing a systematic understand­
ing of LLM applications in urban planning through comprehensive literature review and 
conceptual framework development. Building on established traditions in planning 
theory that emphasize the importance of reflective practice and communicative ration­
ality (Healey, 2006; Allmendinger, 2017), this research seeks to provide both practical 
guidance for LLM integration and critical analysis of broader implications for planning 
practice.
Specifically, this research contributes to emerging scholarship on AI urbanism by 
focusing on the particular characteristics and requirements of LLMs in urban planning 
contexts, developing a conceptual framework that addresses both technical capabilities 
and professional considerations, and providing recommendations for responsible inte­
gration of these technologies into planning practice.
This systematic review is guided by three interrelated research questions that address 
both technical and theoretical dimensions of LLM integration in urban planning: 
. What are the existing applications of LLMs in urban planning literature?
. What key characteristics should LLMs possess to effectively address the complex chal­
lenges inherent in urban planning practice?
. How can a conceptual framework be developed to guide the application and advance­
ment of LLMs in urban planning research and practice?
The study employs a systematic literature review methodology following the Preferred 
Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to 
ensure comprehensive coverage and methodological rigor. The analysis synthesizes 
findings across multiple dimensions, including technical capabilities, application 
domains, professional considerations, and broader implications for planning theory 
and practice.
Research Design
This research follows the PRISMA protocol to ensure the systematicity and transparency 
of the literature review. The PRISMA protocol provides a clear framework for database 
searches, selection of relevant literature, and information synthesis, thereby ensuring the 
comprehensiveness and reproducibility of the research process. The methodology of this 
review encompasses formulating keywords, execution of a systematic search, application 
of inclusion and exclusion criteria, literature screening, and analysis and synthesis of the 
research data.

To comprehensively explore the application of LLMs in urban planning, this research 
developed a set of keywords designed to cover relevant research areas. Based on the 
research questions, the keywords were categorized into two groups: topic-related 
terms and method-related terms. 
▪ Topic-related terms: These were used to retrieve studies related to urban planning, 
including urban, city, town, and regional planning.
▪ Method-related terms: These were used to retrieve studies related to LLMs, including 
the generative per-trained transformer (GPT), LLM.
Each keyword string variant was adjusted according to the search functionalities of 
different databases to ensure the retrieval of the most relevant and consistent results. 
Given that the application of LLMs in urban planning is still in its early stages, this 
search did not set a publication date range to avoid omitting significant early literature.
The literature search utilized Scopus and Web of Science (WoS) databases. To further 
optimize the search process, Boolean operators AND and OR were employed. The AND 
operator was used to combine multiple topics to ensure the search results included 
studies spanning both LLMs and urban applications. The OR operator expanded the 
search scope by introducing synonyms and related terms, capturing a broader range of 
relevant literature. Based on the search keywords, the study initially retrieved 1,379 
articles from Web of Science and Scopus. After removing duplicates and conducting a 
title screening, the results were reduced to 174 articles. Following a detailed review of 
the titles based on inclusion criteria, the final selection was narrowed down to 58 articles 
(See Figure 1 and Supplementary Material Table S1).
To ensure that the selected literature is closely aligned with the application of LLMs in 
urban planning, the following inclusion and exclusion criteria were established to main­
tain a clear research focus and ensure the quality and relevance of the literature.
Inclusion Criteria: 
▪ The study explores the application of LLMs in urban, city, town, or regional planning.
▪ The study presents case studies, experimental results, or analyses of real-world appli­
cations where LLMs are used to address specific urban planning problems.
▪ The literature provides a clear methodological description, including the use, optimization, 
or innovation of LLM technologies, with sufficient theoretical or practical support.
Exclusion Criteria: 
▪ Studies that focus solely on other AI technologies unrelated to LLMs.
▪ Studies that only discuss traditional urban planning methods and tools without invol­
ving LLM technologies.
▪ Studies that focus solely on LLM development or technical improvements without 
applications in urban planning contexts.
▪ Studies that lack sufficient methodological support or fail to clearly demonstrate the 
real impact of LLMs on urban planning.
▪ Non-peer-reviewed publications or research reports that do not provide detailed appli­
cation scenarios and results.

These criteria were strictly applied throughout our screening process. For example, we 
excluded studies that only used traditional computer vision methods to analyze urban 
traffic patterns and predict congestion, as they did not involve LLM technologies 
despite being relevant to urban planning. We also excluded studies focusing purely on 
LLM development, such as those proposing new pre-training strategies or architectural 
improvements for enhanced language understanding, as they lacked specific applications 
in urban planning contexts. The application of these criteria ensured that our analysis 
remained focused specifically on advanced LLM applications within urban planning con­
texts. While our reliance on Web of Science and Scopus databases may not capture all 
technical developments in LLMs, particularly those presented at computer science con­
ferences, this approach aligns with our focus on understanding LLM applications specifi­
cally within urban planning contexts.

Analysis and Results
General Observations
Based on the data extracted from the reviewed articles (n = 58), analysis reveals that 
research on LLMs in urban planning began to increase in 2023, with further growth in 
2024. Prior to 2023, studies related to LLMs were primarily focused on language 
models (LMs) and NLP, with a particular focus on applications in chatbot systems, 
healthcare, and finance (Meng, 2021; Chang and Bergen, 2024). During this earlier 
period, researchers concentrated on improving the language comprehension capabilities 
of models, addressing challenges such as text generation, sentiment analysis, and 
machine translation (Wei et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2023; Sahebi, 2024; Minaee et al., 2024).
The increase in LLM research in urban planning after 2023 relates to the complex data 
characteristics of this domain. Urban planning requires processing of multiple data types, 
including sensor data, geographic information, and real-time video streams, which were 
beyond the capabilities of earlier LMs (De Zarzà et al., 2023; Blečić et al., 2024; Guan 
et al., 2024). Furthermore, the complexities associated with cross-technology integration 
and the stringent real-time data processing requirements in urban management applications 
further constrained the scope of early research efforts (Guan et al., 2024; Li, Xia et al., 2024).
The introduction of advanced models like GPT-4 in 2023 improved LLMs’ capabilities 
in multimodal data processing and dynamic analysis (Josh et al., 2023). This led to 
expanded applications in smart cities, traffic management, and environmental monitor­
ing, extending research beyond traditional NLP tasks to cross-disciplinary applications. 
At the same time, the maturation of IoT and edge computing technologies has facilitated 
the integration of LLMs with these systems, creating new opportunities for urban plan­
ning and automated management.
LLM Research Proportion in the Field of Urban Planning
The first stage of our data extraction process employed inductive thematic analysis 
methods (Braun and Clarke, 2006). For each included study, we conducted open 
reading and coding to identify primary research focus and application contexts. 
Through iterative reading and comparison, we inductively identified twenty distinct 
specific application themes, which emerged directly from literature content, representing 
actual LLM application domains within urban planning contexts. The twenty themes

were then grouped into five broader urban planning domains through similarity cluster­

Through systematic analysis of these five domains, our research reveals that LLM 
applications are concentrated in planning and management (n = 20) and transportation 
and urban mobility (n = 19), with these two domains collectively accounting for over 67 
percent of all identified applications. This concentration reflects a fundamental align­
ment between LLM technological capabilities and the specific characteristics of infor­
mation-intensive planning domains.
Information Processing Challenges and Computational Planning Problems. Contem­
porary urban planning faces what scholars term the “knowledge integration problem,” 
where institutional analytical capacity struggles to match the complexity of available 
information (Healey, 2006; Allmendinger, 2017). Planning and management activities 
require synthesizing exponentially growing data volumes from diverse sources including 
regulatory frameworks, demographic data, economic indicators, and stakeholder 
opinions, while maintaining decision coherence and democratic accountability. This 
challenge has become central to AI urbanism discourse, representing a paradigmatic 
shift from automation-oriented smart cities toward more autonomous, adaptive analyti­
cal capabilities (Cugurullo, 2020).
Within this context, the information-intensive characteristics of the planning and 
management domain have fostered LLM applications focused on knowledge integration 
and processing. As shown in Table 2, intelligent planning systems (n = 9) emerged as the 
highest-proportion application theme in this domain, reflecting LLM technological 
advantages in addressing large-scale, multi-source data synthesis and analysis needs. 
The development of systems such as CityGPT’s multi-agent urban IoT integration 
(Guan et al., 2024) and UrbanLLM’s autonomous activity planning (Jiang et al., 2024) 
indicates that LLMs are being shaped as cognitive enhancement tools specifically 
designed to handle complex information integration tasks that traditional methods 
struggle to address.
The emergence of policy analysis and development support (n = 6) as another cluster 
reflects the necessity for LLMs to balance technical analytical capabilities with policy 
comprehensibility. The development trajectory from ChatGPT’s planning evaluation 
capabilities (Valmeekam et al., 2023) to transparent urban management frameworks 
(Wang and Zhong, 2024) shows that LLM applications are compelled to develop stronger 
interpretability and transparency functions while maintaining analytical depth.
Temporal Dynamics and Real-time Adaptation Requirements as Technology Drivers. 
Unlike the relatively static analysis of planning and management, transportation’s 
demand for continuous data stream processing and instant decision-making has 
driven LLM evolution toward real-time analysis and dynamic optimization (Batty, 2018).

Human-Centered Challenges. However, as perspectives shift from technology-oriented 
to human-centered approaches, LLM applications face more complex challenges. AI 
urbanism raises fundamental questions about power structures and democratic govern­
ance in urban contexts, particularly regarding how algorithmic systems mediate relation­
ships between citizens and planning institutions (Bratton, 2021). When examining 
domains requiring more complex human-machine interaction, we find that public ser­
vices and citizen participation (n = 13), environmental monitoring and sustainability 
(n = 11), and urban design and architecture (n = 6) present challenges that extend 
beyond computational problem-solving to require deep integration of contextual intelli­

necessitate that LLMs in public services develop specific adaptive functions. Research 
by Fu et al. (2024) on participatory urban planning and Zhou, Lin et al. (2024) on

cultural adaptation, and bias mitigation in this domain—capabilities that are relatively 
less important in technology-oriented domains. Simultaneously, democratic partici­
pation’s demand for humanistic characteristics makes LLM applications in this domain 
more manifestations of auxiliary tools rather than independent decision-making systems.

Overview of LLM Technology Domains in Urban Planning
To systematically analyze the technological foundations underlying LLM applications in 
urban planning, we employed inductive content analysis methods (Schreier, 2012; 
Erlingsson and Brysiewicz, 2017). In this review, we define LLM techniques as a compre­
hensive collection of underlying model architectures, algorithmic mechanisms, and 
engineering implementation methods that support LLMs in executing semantic under­
standing, language generation, knowledge management, multimodal processing, and 
human-computer interaction functions in practical applications. This encompasses 
both general language capabilities at the model level and functional technical modules 
with system integration mechanisms constructed for specific tasks.
For each study, we focused on two key aspects. First, we extracted the actual appli­
cation effects and implementation results of LLMs reported in the literature. Second, 
we identified the specific LLM technologies and methods employed to achieve these 
application outcomes. Subsequently, the extracted technologies were categorized into 
eight technology domains based on functional similarity (Figure 3). The following analy­
sis examines each domain’s specific role and relative prominence in urban planning 
applications, revealing how different technological capabilities address distinct aspects 
of urban planning complexity.

Essential Characteristics of LLMs
The identification of LLM characteristics follows a systematic inductive approach that 
abstracts core qualities from observed application manifestations. This analytical 
process moves beyond describing what LLMs do in urban planning contexts to concep­
tualizing the fundamental characteristics that enable their effectiveness across diverse 
planning scenarios.
Our analysis employed inductive content analysis (Schreier, 2012; Erlingsson and Bry­
siewicz, 2017) to systematically examine how LLMs perform in practice across the 
reviewed literature. This examination revealed eleven distinct manifestations of LLM 
capabilities, including accuracy in analytical tasks, flexibility in adapting to different 
scenarios, efficiency in processing complex information, and transparency in decision- 
making processes. These manifestations, derived entirely from empirical descriptions 
and evaluations of LLM performance in the literature, represent the observable qualities 
through which LLMs demonstrate their value in urban planning applications.
Through systematic analysis and conceptual clustering of these manifestations, follow­
ing grounded theory’s axial coding principles (Charmaz, 2014), four core characteristics 
emerged: professionalism, inclusivity, trustworthiness, and convenience (See Table 15). 
Each characteristic represents a conceptual aggregation of related manifestations, captur­
ing different dimensions of the fundamental qualities required for LLMs to function 
effectively in urban planning contexts.
These characteristics not only facilitate a systematic understanding of the application 
value of LLMs in urban planning but also provide a clear framework for future research 
and the development of LLMs. The sections that follow delve into the specifics of these 
four key characteristics, analyzing their concrete applications and significance in the 
context of urban planning.

Professionalism. Professionalism emerges as a core characteristic of LLMs through sys­
tematic analysis of their demonstrated professional competence in urban planning appli­
cations. This characteristic was inductively derived by examining multiple manifestations 
of LLM performance across the 58 reviewed studies, where these models consistently dis­
played domain-specific expertise, analytical rigor, and adherence to professional stan­
dards. The analysis reveals that professionalism manifests through three key 
dimensions that collectively define LLMs’ professional competence in urban planning 
contexts: accuracy, authority, and comprehensiveness (See Table 16).
Within the dimension of professionalism, accuracy emerges as a fundamental mani­
festation that reflects how effectively LLMs align their performance with real-world 
urban planning requirements. This manifestation is particularly critical given the 
inherent complexity of urban planning tasks, such as spatio-temporal data processing, 
regulatory interpretation, and spatial layout design, which demand precise analytical 
capabilities and multidimensional data management (Cugurullo and Xu, 2024; Jiao 
et al., 2024).
The importance of accuracy is highlighted across various studies. For instance, in pro­
cessing spatio-temporal data generated by the IoT, the high accuracy of LLMs is critical for 
ensuring the reliability of real-time analysis and decision making (Guan et al., 2024). Simi­
larly, studies by Sarzaeim et al. (2024) and Yao (2023) highlight the advantages of LLMs in 
managing large volumes of real-time data. However, these studies also emphasize the 
trade-off between precision and speed in dynamic environments, noting that LLMs 
must maintain high accuracy to support rapid decision making. Further research indicates 
that by fine-tuning algorithms for spatio-temporal tasks, LLMs can provide more precise 
recommendations for autonomous urban planning and management (Jiang et al., 2024).

Inclusivity. Inclusivity emerges as a distinctive characteristic that addresses the social 
dimension of LLM effectiveness in urban planning contexts. While professionalism 
focuses on technical competence and adherence to standards, inclusivity reflects LLMs’ 
capacity to bridge diverse communities and contexts within urban environments. This 
characteristic was identified through examining how LLMs demonstrate adaptive capa­
bilities across varied user groups, linguistic communities, and cultural contexts in the 
reviewed literature.
The analysis reveals that inclusivity manifests through two interconnected dimen­
sions: diversity and fairness (See Table 17). These dimensions capture how LLMs 
extend beyond technical functionality to address the social complexity inherent in 
urban planning, encompassing capabilities such as multilingual processing, multimodal 
data handling, personalized response generation, and algorithmic bias mitigation. 
Together, these manifestations reflect LLMs’ potential to democratize access to planning 
processes and ensure equitable representation across diverse urban communities.
The first dimension of inclusivity becomes evident through LLMs’ capacity to embrace 
diversity across urban planning environments, particularly in addressing the varied needs 
of heterogeneous user populations (Li, Haider et al., 2024; Otal et al., 2024). Given the 
multilingual nature of urban environments often results in communication barriers, 
with the needs of different linguistic communities either overlooked or inadequately 
expressed (Cadier and Mar-Molinero, 2014; Otal et al., 2024; Zhou, Lin et al., 2024). 
To overcome this challenge, LLMs must be capable of processing multilingual inputs 
to support users from various linguistic backgrounds (Wong et al., 2023). Studies 
suggest that through multilingual processing techniques, LLMs can convert resident 
feedback into a unified text format, enabling planners to gather insights from diverse lin­
guistic communities (Wong et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2024; Zhou, Lin et al., 2024). This multi­
lingual capability not only reduces language barriers but also ensures that the 
perspectives of all linguistic groups are considered equally in the planning process.
Beyond linguistic diversity, different communities have varied needs and methods of 
information expression. Planners must extract information that reflects cultural differ­
ences, and evolving community needs from various data formats, such as text, images,

diverse contexts.
While most existing research has focused on model performance and application

research on fairness, it remains a fundamental component of the inclusivity characteristic 
of LLMs.

inclusivity ensures broad accessibility, trustworthiness determines whether stakeholders 
can confidently rely on LLM outputs for critical planning decisions. This characteristic 
was identified through analyzing how LLMs build and maintain credibility across 
diverse planning scenarios in the reviewed literature.

While reliability focuses on consistent performance, transparency addresses the 
important challenge of making LLM decision-making processes accessible to users. 
Urban planning frequently involves the processing of multidimensional data and the 
provision of complex decision support. When users can clearly comprehend the reason­

the models’ recommendations (Cugurullo and Xu, 2024; Esquinas et al., 2024; Šturm

Findings and Discussion
Key Study Insights
This study systematically reviewed the literature to investigate the application of LLMs in 
urban planning, identifying several key characteristics and challenges. The empirical

analysis reveals a rapid expansion of LLM research in urban planning, particularly accel­
erating since 2023, which reflects growing recognition of their potential to enhance 
decision-making and data processing capabilities in complex urban environments. 
This surge aligns with broader developments in AI urbanism, where cities increasingly 
adopt autonomous and adaptive technologies that extend beyond traditional smart 
city approaches (Cugurullo, 2020).
From a domain perspective, our systematic analysis identified five primary application 
areas where LLMs demonstrate particular effectiveness: planning and management, 
public services and participation, transportation and urban mobility, environment and 
sustainability, and urban design and architecture. These domains represent the core 
functions where LLMs’ natural language processing capabilities address longstanding 
challenges in urban planning practice. Furthermore, through our inductive analysis of 
application manifestations, we identified four essential characteristics that enable LLM 
effectiveness across these domains: professionalism, inclusivity, trustworthiness, and 
convenience. These characteristics emerge not as isolated technical features, but as inter­
connected qualities that collectively address the multifaceted needs and complex chal­
lenges inherent in contemporary urban planning practice.
Based on these findings, we conceptualize the role of LLMs in urban planning as 
advanced socio-technical systems capable of managing multi-source and multimodal 
data. These models are intended to facilitate cross-disciplinary decision making by inte­
grating diverse data sources and addressing complex urban challenges, representing a 
shift toward more adaptive and responsive urban planning processes that can better 
accommodate the dynamic nature of contemporary cities while maintaining democratic 
accountability and professional rigor.
Synergistic Effects of LLM Characteristics Across Urban Planning Domains
Our systematic analysis reveals that LLM characteristics do not operate in isolation but 
demonstrate complex synergistic relationships that vary across different urban planning 
domains. Rather than functioning as independent attributes, the four identified charac­
teristics—professionalism, inclusivity, trustworthiness, and convenience—interact dyna­
mically to create enhanced effectiveness in specific planning contexts. This finding 
emerges from our inductive examination of how these characteristics manifest and 
reinforce each other within the reviewed literature, suggesting that successful LLM 
implementation depends not merely on individual characteristic optimization, but on 
understanding their contextual interdependencies. The analysis further indicates that 
while all characteristics contribute to LLM effectiveness across domains, certain charac­

specific planning areas, reflecting the distinct operational requirements and stakeholder 
dynamics that characterize different aspects of urban planning practice.

characteristic streamlines these traditionally time-consuming tasks through automated

Evaluating the Potential and Limitations of LLMs in Urban Planning
Although LLMs exhibit immense potential in urban planning applications, current 
research remains largely exploratory, lacking sufficient discussion on whether these tools 
truly align with specific use cases. Drawing from past experiences, such as PSS and com­
puter-aided planning, we have seen that an over-reliance on such new tools can result in 
the ‘law of the instrument’ effect—treating all planning problems as solvable by a single 
tool (Matti et al., 2017). This caution should similarly extend to the application of LLMs.
Despite some preliminary successes in areas such as urban governance and intelligent 
transportation systems, the adaptability of LLMs in urban planning remains limited. 
These limitations reflect broader challenges in the evolution from smart cities to AI 
urbanism, where autonomous decision-making capabilities must navigate complex 
urban contexts (Caprotti et al., 2024). From a technical component perspective, 
current research reveals specific performance disparities across different LLM implemen­
tations. For instance, while text understanding and generation technologies perform 
effectively in policy document analysis and citizen feedback processing, they struggle

with the semantic complexity and domain-specific terminology characteristic of urban 
planning discourse (Sharan et al., 2023; Bhandari et al., 2024).
Similarly, knowledge management and learning capabilities demonstrate success in 
retrieving planning regulations and historical cases but exhibit limitations when integrat­
ing contradictory or incomplete information sources commonly encountered in planning 
practice (Arrotta et al., 2024; Zhang, Fu et al., 2024,). Models such as TrafficGPT and City- 
LEO have demonstrated the advantages of LLMs in traffic signal control and urban man­
agement, succeeding primarily because these applications involve structured data 
environments with clear performance metrics and limited stakeholder complexity (Jiao 
et al., 2024; Zhang, Fu et al., 2024). Nevertheless, when these models are applied to 
larger, more complex urban environments, their performance is often constrained by 
three primary categories of challenges that reflect both technical limitations and 
broader socio-institutional complexities inherent in urban planning contexts.
First, LLMs struggle with real-time data integration and synchronization across 
different urban systems, reflecting fundamental limitations in multimodal integration 
and cross-domain integration technologies that are essential for urban planning appli­
cations. Current data processing and analysis capabilities cannot adequately handle the 
temporal complexity and format heterogeneity characteristic of dynamic urban environ­
ments, as evidenced in transportation management scenarios, where the models face 
difficulties in simultaneously processing real-time traffic sensor data, historical traffic 
patterns, and urban event information to make timely decisions (De Zarzà et al., 
2023). Similarly, existing multimodal integration approaches remain insufficient for 
managing the scale and diversity of real-time urban data streams, demonstrated by chal­
lenges in environmental monitoring, where LLMs must integrate diverse data types from 
various sources, such as air quality sensors and satellite imagery, while maintaining data 
consistency and accuracy (Guan et al., 2024).
While these integration challenges might appear novel, they reflect a long-standing 
evolution in urban planning tools. Traditional GIS like ArcGIS (Yeh, 1999) and early 
urban modeling tools like CUBE and EMME/2 approached data integration through pre­
defined, structured workflows with clear data hierarchies (Boyce, 2002). These systems 
excelled in processing structured spatial data but were limited by their inability to 
handle unstructured information. Decision support systems like What if? and Commu­
nityViz similarly relied on explicit rule-based processes (Klosterman and Akron, 2001; 
Lieske and Hamerlinck, 2015), offering reliable performance within designed parameters 
but lacking flexibility for dynamic data integration. LLMs attempt to overcome these his­
torical limitations through more flexible data processing capabilities, yet the challenge of 
maintaining data consistency and accuracy across diverse, real-time data streams remains 
a significant technical hurdle that highlights the ongoing limitations of current technol­
ogy domains in addressing the temporal and semantic complexity inherent in contem­
porary urban systems.
Second, the scalability of LLM applications presents significant challenges when 
moving from district-level to city-wide implementations, revealing limitations in intelli­
gent systems and simulation capabilities that are critical for comprehensive urban plan­
ning. LLMs continue to face technical challenges in maintaining consistent performance 
and accuracy when processing complex, interrelated datasets at larger scales, particularly 
when knowledge management and learning systems must handle the multi-level, multi-

of larger urban systems (Liang et al., 2024). Indicating that current technology domains

This scalability issue presents an interesting contrast to earlier urban simulation

lenge in this paradigmatic transition toward more integrated urban intelligence systems.
Another significant issue is that the social and ethical adaptability of LLMs has not yet 
received sufficient attention. This is reflected in the lack of research in related areas, par­
ticularly in discussions surrounding transparency, fairness, and ethical responsibility. 
Studies have shown that LLMs may exhibit bias when handling socially sensitive 
issues, especially in multilingual or multicultural contexts (Fu et al., 2024; Li, Haider 
et al., 2024; Wang, Jiang et al., 2024). For instance, research reveals that LLMs trained 
on data from predominantly developed urban areas may generate planning recommen­
dations that overlook the unique challenges and solutions found in informal settlements 
or developing regions (Cugurullo and Xu, 2024). In multilingual environments, these 
models often show varying levels of comprehension and response quality across 
different language groups, potentially leading to unequal access to urban services and 
participation opportunities (Zhou, Lin et al., 2024).
In the field of urban planning, which involves public and multiple stakeholder inter­
ests, technological decisions need not only to be effective but also to ensure fairness and 
transparency (Cugurullo and Xu, 2024; Jiao et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024a; Zhou, Lin 
et al., 2024). This becomes particularly critical in applications such as public resource 
allocation or community development planning. For example, when processing public 
feedback for urban renewal projects, LLMs might inadvertently prioritize inputs from 
technologically proficient groups while underrepresenting communities with limited 
digital access (Jiao et al., 2024). This challenge of equitable representation recalls 
earlier experiences with participatory planning tools like Public Participation GIS 
(PPGIS) and Geodesign platforms.
While these traditional tools faced accessibility barriers due to their technical com­
plexity, they provided explicit, traceable decision-making processes that users could 
understand and verify (Brown and Kyttä, 2014). Similarly, early sketch planning tools 
like INDEX and CommunityViz emphasized transparency through visual feedback 
and clear analytical rules (Lieske and Hamerlinck, 2015). In contrast, LLMs offer more 
intuitive interaction through natural language processing, potentially broadening

Existing research provides a broad outline of LLMs’ applications yet ensuring their 
effectiveness in broader and more complex contexts will require future studies to 
possess several key characteristics. First, research should further enhance LLMs’ ability 
to process multimodal data, enabling the integration of real-time information from 
diverse sources such as visual, linguistic, and geospatial data, thus providing more accu­
rate decision-making support in complex urban environments (De Zarzà et al., 2023; 
Bhandari et al., 2024; Guan et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024). This improvement would 
enhance LLMs’ adaptability and flexibility when handling complex scenarios (Jiao 
et al., 2024; Wang and Zhong, 2024; Zeng et al., 2024).
Moreover, there is a need to develop stronger localization capabilities for LLMs. This 
entails optimizing global models based on regional cultural, linguistic, and socioeco­
nomic contexts, thereby mitigating the biases currently observed in multilingual and 
multicultural environments (Chen, Yu et al., 2024; Li, Haider et al., 2024; Zhou, Lin 
et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024). In addition to these technical improvements, future research 
should emphasize transparency and accountability mechanisms. This is essential to 
ensure that, when applied in public decision making and governance, the models can 
be widely monitored and understood by society (Cugurullo and Xu, 2024; Zhou, Lin 
et al., 2024). Developing more transparent algorithms and interpretive tools would 
allow the decision-making logic of LLMs to be clearly traceable, which could enhance 
public trust and ensure fairness in practical applications.
Furthermore, as the application of LLMs in socially sensitive issues becomes more 
prevalent, it is crucial to incorporate ethical and fairness frameworks into their design. 
This would ensure that their use in public policy and civic engagement consistently 
aligns with standards of fairness and ethics (Bozkir et al., 2024; Li, Haider et al., 2024; 
Zhou, Lin et al., 2024). Such frameworks would help prevent misuse and decision- 
making biases, safeguarding the fairness of AI-driven decisions for all societal groups.
The analysis of these potentials and limitations in LLM applications provides impor­
tant insights for developing systematic approaches to urban planning integration. The 
demonstrated capabilities in data processing, multimodal integration, and real-time 
decision support suggest the possibility of progressive technological development 
paths. Meanwhile, the current limitations in technical adaptability and social-ethical con­
siderations emphasize the importance of incorporating robust optimization mechanisms 
in future implementations. These findings collectively point toward the need for a struc­
tured framework that can guide both the technical advancement and practical implemen­
tation of LLMs in urban planning, while ensuring their effectiveness across various 
planning domains.
Towards a Conceptual Framework of LLM in Urban Planning
Based on a systematic literature review, we recognize that the four key characteristics of 
LLMs—professionalism, inclusivity, trustworthiness, and convenience—are not isolated or 
parallel attributes but are interdependent. These characteristics function synergistically 
to ensure the effectiveness of LLMs in various urban planning scenarios. By integrating

these features, LLMs are capable of handling complex planning tasks, addressing both 
data processing and decision-making demands. With this hierarchical understanding,

feedback mechanism, the framework demonstrates how LLMs can evolve from basic

across different roles within the urban planning domain.
In the initial stage of evolution, LLMs function primarily as Knowledge Managers, 
utilizing pre-trained models and domain-specific knowledge to assist planners in 
managing and retrieving vast amounts of urban planning knowledge and regulations. 
For instance, Zhu et al. (2024) demonstrated LLMs’ effectiveness in organizing and 
processing massive planning policies and regulatory documents through semantic 
analysis techniques, substantially reducing planners’ information retrieval time. 
Studies by Chen, Yao et al. (2024) and Li, Xia et al. (2024) documented the systematic 
capabilities of LLMs in categorizing planning cases and historical decisions based on 
multiple attributes, including context relevance and regulatory alignment, enabling 
more efficient application of knowledge in new planning scenarios. This indicates 
that as a Knowledge Manager, this stage shows potential in addressing the knowledge 
integration challenges, particularly in synthesizing fragmented planning knowledge 
and complex regulatory frameworks that traditionally require extensive professional 
experience to navigate.
However, this role also reveals important constraints. While demonstrating high pro­
fessionalism in knowledge organization and retrieval, its effectiveness remains limited to 
static, text-based information. The model struggles with incorporating dynamic urban 
information and cross-domain knowledge integration (Toetzke et al., 2023), indicating 
the need for more advanced capabilities to address complex urban planning challenges. 
Such constraints in static data processing reveal the need for more dynamic capabilities, 
propelling the development of advanced LLM functionalities.
With advancing capabilities in natural language processing and computation, LLMs 
evolve into a Diverse Data Platform role. At this stage, LLMs demonstrate enhanced 
ability to integrate and process heterogeneous urban data, including textual, visual, 
and spatial information. For example, Wang, Pang et al. (2024) demonstrated LLMs’ 
capability in synthesizing diverse data sources including citizen feedback, spatial plan­
ning data and policy documents to generate comprehensive urban development insights 
through integrated analysis techniques. The research by Liang et al. (2024) and Zhang, Fu 
et al. (2024) further illustrates LLMs’ capability to process multimodal urban data, effec­
tively analyzing urban images alongside textual descriptions to support planning 
decisions. This indicates that as a Diverse Data Platform, this stage shows potential in 
addressing the multimodal integration challenges, particularly in bridging the gap 
between different data formats and sources that traditionally exist in isolated systems. 
Through improved inclusivity and data processing capabilities, LLMs at this stage can 
help planners better understand the multifaceted nature of urban environments. 
However, while this role represents significant progress in data integration, it still faces

requires further advancement in human-AI interaction and ethical decision-making 
frameworks. While some systems demonstrate promising capabilities in controlled 
environments, fully realizing this role depends on continued progress in multimodal 
AI, cross-cultural adaptation, and transparent decision-making processes.

The analysis of these four developmental roles reveals that the effectiveness and evol­
ution of each role depends heavily on learning from practical implementations. From the 
Knowledge Manager’s need to refine information retrieval processes to the Collaborative 
Planning Expert’s requirement for adaptive interaction capabilities, the necessity for sys­
tematic feedback becomes evident. This recognition of feedback’s importance across all 
roles emphasizes the feedback and technical optimization mechanism as a critical com­

improvement.
Throughout this evolutionary process, the Feedback and Technical Optimization 
mechanism serves as a critical driving force that connects and enhances all developmen­
tal roles while enabling continuous improvement. Through systematic collection and 
analysis of real-world application data, this component enables LLMs to refine their 
capabilities from basic knowledge management to sophisticated collaborative planning. 
For instance, Yang et al. (2024a) found that continuous feedback from planning practice 
significantly improves LLMs’ capabilities in knowledge organization and multimodal 
processing, resulting in enhanced accuracy and contextual awareness in their responses.
Studies by Wang and Zhong (2024) and Zhou, Lin et al. (2024) further revealed that 
user interaction data and implementation outcomes play a crucial role in strengthening 
LLMs’ decision-making processes and social adaptability. This continuous optimization 
process helps address several limitations identified in the framework. It improves knowl­
edge retrieval accuracy, enhances data integration capabilities, optimizes real-time 
responses, and refines collaborative interactions across the various roles LLMs can 
play in urban planning. The feedback mechanism thus ensures that LLMs can progress­
ively enhance their performance in complex urban environments, learning from practical 
applications to better serve diverse planning needs. However, implementing effective 
feedback systems presents its own challenges. Research by Li, Xia et al. (2024) and 
Guan et al. (2024) highlights that maintaining data quality and ensuring representative 
feedback across different urban contexts requires careful consideration. These findings 
emphasize that while the feedback mechanism is essential for advancing LLM capabilities 
across all roles, its effectiveness depends on systematic implementation and continuous 
refinement of optimization processes.
Conclusion
This systematic review has addressed a significant knowledge gap in our understanding 
of how LLMs are transforming urban planning practice. Through analyzing 58 carefully 
selected studies, we have traced the emergence of a new technological paradigm that 
extends far beyond traditional planning support systems to encompass sophisticated 
AI-driven capabilities that promise to reshape how cities are planned, managed, and 
governed.
The analysis reveals that LLM applications in urban planning are not simply about 
technological efficiency—they represent a fundamental shift toward more adaptive, 
responsive, and potentially democratic planning processes. Our identification of five 
key application domains demonstrates how LLMs are addressing longstanding chal­
lenges across the full spectrum of planning activities, from strategic management and 
public participation to environmental monitoring and urban design. Perhaps most

significantly, the four characteristics we identified—professionalism, inclusivity, trust­
worthiness, and convenience—emerge not as isolated technical features but as intercon­
nected qualities that collectively address the complex, multifaceted nature of 
contemporary urban challenges.
This research makes important advances to urban planning theory by providing the 
first comprehensive framework for understanding AI integration as an evolutionary 
process rather than a simple technology adoption decision. The four-stage developmental 
model we propose, ranging from Knowledge Managers to Collaborative Planning 
Experts, offers planning theory its first systematic approach to analyzing how emerging 
technologies mature within professional practice contexts. This framework addresses a 
critical gap in planning theory regarding the relationship between technological capa­
bility development and institutional readiness.
Our analysis reveals that LLM effectiveness emerges from the complex interplay 
between different characteristics rather than from any single technical capability. The 
synergistic relationships we identified between professionalism, inclusivity, trustworthi­
ness, and convenience across different planning domains demonstrate that successful AI 
integration requires understanding how these qualities reinforce each other in specific 
planning contexts. This finding suggests that future LLM development should move 
beyond optimizing individual features toward creating systems that balance multiple 
characteristics simultaneously.
The practical implications of this research extend well beyond academic understand­
ing. For planning organizations grappling with whether and how to integrate AI systems, 
our characteristic framework provides a systematic evaluation tool that can guide tech­
nology selection and implementation strategies. Rather than being overwhelmed by tech­
nical specifications, planners can assess whether specific LLM implementations possess 
the necessary combination of characteristics for their intended applications, such as 
prioritizing professionalism and convenience for analytical tasks, or inclusivity and con­
venience for public participation processes.
For policymakers, our findings highlight critical areas requiring immediate atten­
tion. The analysis reveals a concerning imbalance in current LLM development tra­
jectories: while research heavily emphasizes technical applications focused on 
efficiency and automation, there is insufficient attention to security, privacy, 
ethical considerations, and democratic participation. This concentration of research 
in technical applications versus the limited attention to governance concerns reveals 
significant gaps that could undermine public trust in AI-assisted planning and poses 
risks to the participatory and equity-focused values that define contemporary plan­
ning practice. The scalability challenges we identified further suggest that successful 
LLM deployment requires not just technological advancement but also institutional 
capacity building and regulatory frameworks that can evolve alongside the 
technology.
Looking ahead, this research points toward the need for what we might call “respon­
sible AI urbanism,” an approach that harnesses the powerful capabilities of LLMs while 
maintaining the democratic, participatory, and equity-focused values that define good 
planning practice. This will require not just better technology, but better frameworks 
for ensuring that AI systems serve to enhance rather than replace human judgment in 
planning decisions.
