Measuring Potential Access to Food Stores and
Food-Service Places in Rural Areas in the U.S.
Joseph R. Sharkey, PhD, MPH, RD
Abstract:
Geographic access to healthy food resources remains a major focus of research that
examines the contribution of the built environment to healthful eating. Methods used to
deﬁne and measure spatial accessibility can signiﬁcantly affect the results. Considering the
implications for marketing, policy, and programs, adequate measurement of the food
environment is important. Little of the published work on food access has focused on rural
areas, where the burden of nutrition-related disease is greater. This article seeks to expand
our understanding of the challenges to measurement of potential spatial access to food
resources in rural areas in the U.S. Key challenges to the accurate measurement of the food
environment in rural areas include: (1) deﬁning the rural food environment while
recognizing that market factors may be changing; (2) describing characteristics that may
differentiate similar types of food stores and food-service places; and (3) determining
location coordinates for food stores and food-service places.
In order to enhance measurements in rural areas, “ground-truthed” methodology, which
includes on-site observation and collection of GPS data, should become the standard for
rural areas. Measurement must also recognize the emergence of new and changing store
formats. Efforts should be made to determine accessibility, in terms of both proximity to a
single location and variety of multiple locations within a speciﬁed buffer, from origins
other than the home, and consider multipurpose trips and trip chaining. The measure-
ment of food access will be critical for community-based approaches to meet dietary needs.
Researchers must be willing to take the steps necessary for rigorous measurement of a
dynamic food environment.

Introduction
T
he consumption of nutritious foods is essential
to achieve and maintain good health, and to
prevent and manage nutrition-related health
conditions, such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardio-
vascular disease.1 It is generally accepted that per-
sonal, structural, and neighborhood characteristics
serve as barriers or enhancements to healthful eat-
ing.2–5 In particular, recognition is growing that in
order for individuals to make health-promoting food
choices, low-calorie, nutrient-dense food resources
need to be accessible (located near neighborhoods,
especially low-income or rural areas, or with transpor-
tation available); available (including a variety of low-fat
and nutrient-dense options in local stores); and afford-
able (reasonably priced).6–8 The purpose of this article
is to expand our understanding of potential spatial
access to food stores and food-service places within the
overall food environment in the U.S. to include rural
areas. In so doing, context of the rural food environ-
ment and spatial access will be explained, followed by
challenges to measurement in rural areas. Recommen-
dations will be presented and conclusions drawn.
Context of the Rural Food Environment
The rural food environment includes a wider variety of
food stores and food-service places than commonly
reported in studies of urban or metropolitan areas.9–11
Using modiﬁed versions of the North America Industry
Classiﬁcation System (NAICS) deﬁnitions for the pri-
mary business,12 rural food stores may be classiﬁed as
conventional or traditional food stores, such as super-
centers (e.g., Super Wal-Mart or Super Kmart), super-
markets, grocery stores, and specialty food stores (e.g.,
meat markets, ﬁsh and seafood markets, fruit and
vegetables markets, and stores selling baked goods not
for immediate consumption)9,13; convenience food
stores (e.g., gasoline stations with convenience stores,
convenience stores with gasoline, or food marts); and
nontraditional food store formats, such as drug stores
or pharmacies with food, mass merchandisers (e.g.,

In many rural areas, food sources are not evenly
distributed; there are areas of concentration and areas
where few or no grocery stores exist.9,18 Rural areas are
increasingly being adversely affected by changes in the
food environment. The retail food industry is consoli-
dating, which has led to fewer but larger stores, and in
many rural areas to increased transportation costs,
higher food prices, less variety, and lower-quality fresh
produce and meat compared to suburban and urban
stores.19–21 Consolidation concentrates retail food stores
in regional hub towns; small town food stores, with
limited ﬂoor space, economies of scale, and distance
from distribution centers, continue to provide limited
food selection at higher prices and face the persistent
challenge to remain proﬁtable.18 The loss of small-town
grocery stores, which increases the distances that rural
residents must travel to obtain food, is particularly
critical for low-income families who face continued
threats to food access and food security.22 They have
less independent access to a vehicle, face income
limitations and lack of public transportation, are more
likely to shop less frequently and at one rather than
multiple grocery stores, and must expend greater re-
sources to obtain food through normal sources.18 In
many rural areas, the convenience store is the main
source of food items. As a result, rural families with
only convenience or small stores from which to
choose experience higher prices and lower selection
and quality of foods than those shopping in larger
supermarkets.13,18
Spatial Access
Spatial access from the home to the food environment
(i.e., food stores and food-service places) remains a
major focus of research that examines the contribution
of the built environment to healthy eating.5 There are
several dimensions of accessibility to food resources,
each with its own measure: proximity (distance to the
nearest food store or food-service place)9,23–25; variety
(number of food stores or food-service places within a
speciﬁed distance, such as ½-mile, 1-mile, or 3-miles)
(JRS, unpublished ﬁndings, 2008)23,26; food/price va-
riety (average distance to the three closest different
chain-name supermarkets)23; and density (proportion
or ratio of food stores per county, census tract, or
census block group or population).27,28 Research, con-
ducted primarily with urban data, has shown that
populations with limited or difﬁcult access to a super-
market tend to live in neighborhoods with greater
disadvantages and/or with a higher proportion of
minority residents. Residents of these same neighbor-
hoods tend to consume fewer fruits and vegetables, and
often have a greater BMI than do residents of afﬂuent
areas.7,8,27,29–31 Without easy spatial access to supermar-
kets or full grocery stores, individuals either have to pay
high travel costs to reach a supermarket or grocery
store, or are able to shop only at convenience or small
grocery stores and pay high prices for a limited selec-
tion of food products.10,32–34
Conceptually, potential access (availability) can be
distinguished from realized access (actual use), recog-
nizing that potential access has spatial (geographic)
and nonspatial (e.g., social) dimensions.35 A number of
characteristics of the food environment have been
identiﬁed that inﬂuence potential access and serve as
barriers or facilitators for realized access by users.
These include the number and type of food stores and
food-service places, organization (chain or independent),
size, distribution and location, and distance to the neigh-
borhoods where people reside.30,32,36–38 Methods used to
deﬁne and measure spatial accessibility can consider-
ably affect the results.9,39 Considering the implications
of food-environment access for marketing, policy, and
programs, adequate measurement of the food environ-
ment is important.5,9
It has been recognized that spatial inequality—the
uneven distribution of resources—persists between

Challenges
Key challenges to the accurate measurement of the
food environment in rural areas include:
●deﬁning the rural food environment, recognizing
that market factors (e.g., type of store and offering
of food items) may be changing;
●developing methods to identify all food stores and
food-service places;
●describing characteristics that may differentiate sim-
ilar types of food stores and food-service places; and
●determining locations of food stores and food-
service places.
Collecting Access Data
Determining potential spatial access to the food envi-
ronment relies on two main activities: (1) identifying
store types and locations of retail establishments that
make food available for purchase,5 and (2) geospatially
measuring or geocoding store locations. The built-
environment literature has articulated two main ap-
proaches to carrying out these activities: intermediate
and direct methods.50 Intermediate methods include
the use of historical databases that provide secondary
data from public and commercial sources (e.g., tele-
phone directories, Internet, U.S. Department of Agri-
culture inspection registry, and business listings from
local departments of environmental health, state de-
partments of agriculture, and commercial businesses).
Direct methods include in-person audits or enumeration
completed by trained observers in a systematically
driven (or walked) area and is often referred to as
“ground-truthing.”5,9,50,51
The preponderance of published work on food ac-
cess, which to date has typically targeted urban/metro-
politan areas, has relied primarily on intermediate
methods to identify select types of food stores (e.g.,
supermarkets and grocery stores) and food-service
places (e.g., fast-food restaurants).7,10,23,24,28,33,48,52–55
However, these methods have two major limitations.
First, they may misrepresent (overstate or understate)
the true census of food stores and food-service places
within an area, especially within a rural area. In a recent
study of potential access to food stores in six rural
counties, investigators used ground truthing and com-
pared results with information available from public
databases.9 Almost 19% of the food stores on public
lists did not exist and 35.7% of all food stores were
identiﬁed through ground truthing only. In this case,
exclusive reliance on secondary data for the identiﬁca-
tion of food stores would have signiﬁcantly misrepre-
sented food store access; public lists omitted 26% of
supermarkets or grocery stores, 36% of convenience
stores, and 20% of discount stores.9
In an examination of rural fast-food and full-service
restaurants, more than 20% of the away-from-home
venues were identiﬁed through ground-truthing only.17
In addition to misrepresenting the census of food
stores, use of public lists compared to ground truthing
signiﬁcantly overestimated the distance to the nearest
food store.9 Thus, two measures of access—nearest
location measure and cumulative-opportunity measures
(number of opportunities accessible within a certain
travel distance from a given location)—would be incor-
rect.49 Furthermore, a reliance on intermediate meth-
ods precludes the opportunity to observe food store
and food-service place quality characteristics that may
inﬂuence access. These include number of parking
spaces, availability of gasoline, and other services of-
fered (e.g., money order sales, banking services, and
bill paying).9 Although it is assumed that ground truth-
ing, which does provide this opportunity, is more expen-
sive than using secondary lists, this economic comparison
does not appear in the published literature.
A second major limitation of the intermediate ap-
proach is that it customarily uses a commercial vendor
or software with a street database to geocode the data.
This can result in positional errors and address inaccu-
racies, particularly in rural areas. The quality of geoc-
oding is generally better in urban areas, with fewer
errors in block group–level data when compared to
rural and/or poor areas.56,57 The gold standard is use
of GPS technology to pinpoint exact locations.57 In a
previously-mentioned rural study, on-site GPS was used
to accurately determine geographic coordinates for all
food stores.9
Additional Challenges
Three additional challenges warrant mention. First,
although the food environment is dynamic (e.g., stores
and formats are continuously opening and closing),
food-environment measures are usually made once,
and may be outdated if they come from secondary data.
Even ground truthing at a single time may miss impor-
tant changes, especially if other data are collected at
multiple times. The presence of three nontraditional
formats of food stores is increasing: (1) “big-box” stores
(large, free-standing general merchandise or “category-
killer” stores like Wal-Mart, Home Depot), especially
supercenters, which combine mass merchandise and
full-line grocery; (2) dollar stores; and (3) drug-
stores.58–60 Dollar stores and drug stores have in-

creased their food offerings, with some introducing
refrigerated and frozen sections to their stores, all at
the expense of traditional supermarkets and grocery
stores.59 Rural areas are most affected by these changes
in the food environment. In the case of supercenters,
retail food sales have shifted from counties without
supercenters to those with supercenters, creating what
has been referred to as “food deserts” in some ar-
eas.52,61,62 Especially hard hit are rural populations,
where distance and transportation are critical factors,
especially for those who are poor, disabled, or elderly.52
Unless multiple measures of the food environment are
made over time, many of these changes go unobserved.
A second challenge concerns the selection of loca-
tion point for access. The type of origin and destination
points may differ and produce dramatically different
pictures of access.49 Access to the nearest food store or
food-service place is usually measured from the resi-
dence, neighborhood, or center of an administrative
area (e.g., census tract or census block group). How-
ever, travel to a supermarket may not begin at the
residence: The supermarket may be one part of a
planned multipurpose trip, or travel may begin at work
and involve trip chaining, which could involve stopping
at the supermarket between work and home.49 Thus,
restricting the measure of access to home-based acces-
sibility may incorrectly describe rural food accessibility.
Third, food-environment measures that are limited
by the geographic boundaries may misrepresent access.
For example, in the case of a residence or neighbor-
hood that is located near the edge of a county, the
nearest food store may be physically located in an
adjacent county, which may not be measured. Much the
same could be said for analyses that restrict access to
the same census tract or census block group.24
Recommendations
Carrying out the following recommendations would
greatly enhance measurement of the food environment
in rural areas:
●Ground-truthed methodology, which includes on-
site observation and collection of GPS data, should
become the standard for rural areas.
●The measurement of the food environment must
recognize the emergence of new and changing store
formats.
●Multiple measures of the food environment over
time should be encouraged, especially in studies that
are measuring other variables at multiple times.
●Efforts should be made to determine accessibility
(distance from origin to destination) from origins
other than the home. Home-based measures ex-
clude multipurpose trips and trip chaining.49
●Access should be described in more than one dimen-
sion, such as distance (to the nearest speciﬁc type of
store) and cumulative opportunities or variety (num-
ber of stores within a speciﬁc geographic area).23
●Food-environment researchers need to include data
on availability and price along with distance infor-
mation in their measurements in order to provide
robust meaning to the concept of access.
●Unless researchers are measuring exactly where peo-
ple go to acquire food, the measure of access should
be described as “potential access” and not overall
access.
Conclusion
Rural populations live with the conﬂuence of greater
burden of nutrition-related disease, economic restric-
tions, and greater spatial inequality for access to health-
ful food.40,46 Increased attention must be directed
toward the availability of affordable, nutritious food in
rural areas. The measurement of food access will be
critical for community-based approaches to meet di-
etary needs. Researchers must be willing to take the
steps necessary for rigorous measurement of a dynamic,
food environment. Especially in rural areas, where
individuals may regularly travel to a larger city or town
for employment or other services, the measurement of
access to a food environment (distance between origin
and destination) must take into consideration origins
that are not home-based, but reﬂect multipurpose trips
and trip chaining.
